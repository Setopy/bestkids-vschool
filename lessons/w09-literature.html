✒ Literature · Week 9

Comparative Myth

The big questionPeoples in Mesopotamia, Greece, India and Mexico, who never met, all told of a great flood that drowned the world and of a few survivors in a boat. How can the same story appear on opposite sides of the Earth?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Define motif and identify motifs shared across traditions.
  2. Compare two stories systematically, noting similarities and differences.
  3. Explain three theories for why distant cultures tell similar stories.
  4. Write a comparative paragraph supported by evidence.

1Reading side by side

Comparative mythology studies the stories of different peoples next to each other. A motif is an element that recurs from story to story: a flood, a forbidden box, a trickster, a hero's descent to the underworld. In Week 2 you met the largest recurring pattern of all, the Hero's Journey.

To compare well, ask the same questions of each story.

  • Who acts, and why?
  • What goes wrong, and who is to blame?
  • How is it resolved, and at what cost?
  • What does the story suggest about the divine, about nature, and about how people should live?

The differences are usually more revealing than the likenesses.

The Aztecs of Mexico also told that an earlier world, the fourth "Sun", ended in a flood.

Now look at the differences. In the Mesopotamian version the gods quarrel among themselves, and one of them saves a man against the will of the rest. In Genesis there is one God, the flood is a judgement on wrongdoing, and it ends with a covenant, a solemn promise. Each account reflects the beliefs of the people who kept it. The Mesopotamian story matches a land of violent, unpredictable rivers, which you studied in Week 6.

Tricksters break rules. They are small or weak and defeat the strong by cleverness. They cause chaos, and sometimes bring gifts such as fire or stories. Through them a society can laugh at power and think about its own rules.

5Why do the stories match?

TheoryClaimEvidence for it
Shared experienceAll humans face birth, death, danger and floods, so similar stories arise independentlyFlood stories are commonest among peoples living near rivers and coasts
DiffusionStories travelled with traders, migrants and conquerorsThe Greek and Mesopotamian flood stories share precise details, and the regions traded
Common originRelated peoples inherited stories from shared ancestorsGreek, Norse and Indian myths share sky-father gods, and their languages are related (Week 1 Languages)

Many believers also hold that a flood tradition found among so many peoples preserves the memory of a real event. The theories do not exclude each other, and more than one may be true of a single story.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Write a comparative paragraph on Loki and Anansi.

  1. Similarity: both are tricksters who rely on wit instead of strength, and both cause trouble for the powerful.
  2. Evidence: Loki's schemes endanger the gods and he must then repair the damage. Anansi captures fierce creatures by trickery to win the sky god's stories.
  3. Difference: Loki's tricks grow darker and lead at last to Baldr's death and Ragnarök. Anansi's tricks usually end in laughter and a lesson.
  4. Meaning: the Norse trickster shows how cleverness without loyalty destroys a community. The Akan trickster shows how the weak can outwit the strong.

Answer: "Both Loki and Anansi win by cunning, but while Anansi's cleverness is celebrated as the weapon of the small, Loki's becomes a warning, because it finally destroys the very gods he lives among."

Hands-on: The Myth Matrix

You need: large paper, ruler, coloured pens, a library or online collection of world myths

  1. Choose one motif: creation of the world, the theft of fire, a great flood, or a journey to the land of the dead.
  2. Find the motif in three traditions from three different continents.
  3. Draw a matrix with the three stories across the top and the questions from "Reading side by side" down the side. Fill in every box.
  4. Shade the similarities in one colour and the differences in another.
  5. Write one paragraph that states a claim about what the differences reveal, with evidence from your matrix.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is a motif?

    Answer and why

    An element that recurs in many stories.

    WhyMotifs such as floods, tricksters and forbidden objects let us compare stories from different cultures.

  2. Name the flood survivor in the Epic of Gilgamesh and in the Greek myth.

    Answer and why

    Utnapishtim; Deucalion (with his wife Pyrrha)

    WhyEach tradition has its own survivor who rebuilds the human race.

  3. Give one important difference between the Mesopotamian flood story and the account in Genesis.

    Answer and why

    In the Mesopotamian story many gods disagree. In Genesis one God judges wickedness and afterwards makes a covenant.

    WhyThe difference reflects each people's understanding of the divine.

  4. Name two tricksters from different traditions and one thing they share.

    Answer and why

    Loki and Anansi (or Hermes, or Coyote). They win by cleverness and break rules.

    WhyThe trickster motif appears worldwide as a way to explore the limits of rules and power.

  5. What is the "diffusion" theory of similar myths?

    Answer and why

    Stories spread from one people to another through trade, migration and conquest.

    WhyPeoples in contact borrow tales as readily as they borrow goods and words.

  6. How does the homeland of the Norse differ from that of the Greeks, and how does this appear in their myths?

    Answer and why

    The north was cold, dark and dangerous. Its myths are grimmer and prize courage against certain loss.

    WhyStories reflect the conditions and fears of the people who tell them.

★ Scholar's challenge

Choose two of the three theories for why myths match. For each, describe one piece of evidence that would strengthen it and one that would weaken it.

Answer and why

Example: Diffusion is strengthened if two matching stories share rare, precise details and the peoples traded. It is weakened if they had no possible contact. Shared experience is strengthened if the motif appears among isolated peoples. It is weakened if the match is so detailed that chance is unlikely.

WhyA good answer treats each theory as a hypothesis, as in Science this week, and asks what observations would support or contradict it.

Words to own

Comparative mythology
The study of myths from different cultures side by side.
Motif
A recurring element in stories.
Trickster
A clever rule-breaker who wins by wit.
Covenant
A solemn, binding promise.
Diffusion
The spread of stories or ideas from one culture to another.
Tradition
Beliefs and stories handed down within a people.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • When two stories match, how can you tell whether one was borrowed or both were invented separately?
  • What do the heroes and tricksters a people admire tell you about that people?
  • Which stories of today might be compared across cultures in two thousand years?
