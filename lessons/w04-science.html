⚛ Science · Week 4

The Periodic Table

The big questionIn 1869 a Russian chemist left empty spaces in his chart of the elements and described three elements nobody had ever seen. Within twenty years all three were found, almost exactly as he predicted. How can a table predict the unknown?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Explain how the periodic table is organised by atomic number, periods and groups.
  2. Locate metals, non-metals and metalloids.
  3. Describe the alkali metals, halogens and noble gases.
  4. Link an element's group to its valence electrons.

1Mendeleev's great idea

An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. By the 1860s about sixty were known. Dmitri Mendeleev wrote each on a card and arranged the cards by mass. He saw that properties repeated at regular intervals, in a periodic pattern.

His bold step was to trust the pattern more than the data. Where no known element fitted, he left a gap and predicted the missing element's properties. Gallium was discovered in 1875 and germanium in 1886, both matching his forecasts. A scientific idea earns belief when it makes risky predictions that come true.

2How the modern table is built

Today there are 118 known elements, arranged by atomic number (number of protons).

  • A row is a period. The period number equals the number of electron shells in use.
  • A column is a group. Elements in one group have the same number of valence electrons, so they behave alike.

Compare with last week: sodium is 2, 8, 1. It has three shells (Period 3) and one valence electron (Group 1).

3Three famous families

GroupFamilyValence electronsBehaviour
1Alkali metals (Li, Na, K)1Soft, shiny, react violently with water
17Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I)7Very reactive non-metals; form salts with metals
18Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar)Full outer shellAlmost never react

A full outer shell is stable. Much of chemistry is the story of atoms gaining, losing or sharing electrons to reach that state. That is next week's lesson.

4Metals, non-metals and metalloids

About three quarters of the elements are metals, found on the left and centre. They are shiny, conduct heat and electricity, and can be bent or hammered. Non-metals sit on the right. They are usually dull, brittle or gaseous, and conduct poorly. Along the zigzag border lie the metalloids, such as silicon, with properties in between. Silicon's in-between conduction is why computer chips are made from it.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

An element has the electron arrangement 2, 8, 7. Find its period, group and family, and name it.

  1. Three shells are in use, so it is in Period 3.
  2. Seven valence electrons place it in Group 17, the halogens.
  3. Total electrons = 2 + 8 + 7 = 17, so the atomic number is 17.

Answer: Chlorine: Period 3, Group 17, a halogen

Hands-on: Element Cards

You need: 20 index cards, coloured pencils, a printed periodic table

  1. Make a card for each of the first 20 elements: symbol, name, atomic number and electron arrangement.
  2. Shuffle them. Without looking at the table, lay them out in rows so that elements with the same number of valence electrons line up in columns.
  3. Check against the printed table. You have just repeated Mendeleev's discovery.
  4. Remove one card while a partner looks away. Can they predict the missing element's arrangement and family from its neighbours?
  5. Colour the metals, non-metals and metalloids in three colours.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What does the group number tell you about an element?

    Answer and why

    Its number of valence electrons, and so which elements it resembles.

    WhyElements in one column share an outer-shell pattern, and the outer shell controls chemical behaviour.

  2. Magnesium is 2, 8, 2. Which period and group is it in?

    Answer and why

    Period 3, Group 2

    WhyIt uses three shells and has two valence electrons.

  3. Why are the noble gases so unreactive?

    Answer and why

    Their outer shells are already full.

    WhyAtoms react to reach a full outer shell. Noble gases have nothing to gain by gaining, losing or sharing electrons.

  4. Why is the symbol for gold Au?

    Answer and why

    It comes from the Latin name, aurum.

    WhySeveral elements known since ancient times keep symbols from their Latin names.

  5. Lithium, sodium and potassium all react strongly with water. Explain why they behave alike.

    Answer and why

    All three have one valence electron.

    WhyThey are in Group 1. Each readily gives up its single outer electron, which makes them very reactive.

  6. What made Mendeleev's table more convincing than earlier lists of elements?

    Answer and why

    It predicted undiscovered elements that were later found.

    WhyExplaining what is already known is easy. Correctly forecasting the unknown shows the pattern is real.

★ Scholar's challenge

Element X is in Period 2 and Group 16. Without looking at a table, give its electron arrangement, atomic number and name. Would you expect it to be a metal or a non-metal?

Answer and why

2, 6; atomic number 8; oxygen; a non-metal

WhyPeriod 2 means two shells and Group 16 means six valence electrons. 2 + 6 = 8 protons. Elements at the right of the table are non-metals.

Metalloid

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Mendeleev trusted his pattern enough to say some measured masses must be wrong. When is it reasonable to doubt the data instead of the theory?
  • Is the periodic table a human invention or a discovery about nature?
  • If a new element 119 were made, where would it go, and what would you predict about it?
