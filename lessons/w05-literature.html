Fables & Aesop

The big questionA story of six sentences about a tortoise and a hare has been told for 2,500 years and is known on every continent. Why do the shortest stories last the longest?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Identify the features of a fable.
  2. State the moral of a fable and support it with evidence.
  3. Distinguish a fable from a myth and a parable.
  4. Write an original fable.

1What makes a fable

A fable is a very short story that teaches a lesson about how to live. It has four marks.

  • It is brief: a single scene and a single problem.
  • Its characters are usually animals that behave like people. Giving human qualities to animals or things is anthropomorphism.
  • Each character stands for one trait: the fox is cunning, the ant hard-working, the lion proud.
  • It ends with a moral, a lesson that can be stated in one sentence.

The most famous teller is Aesop, who by tradition was a slave in Greece around 600 BC. He probably wrote nothing down. The fables were collected under his name long afterwards.

Why animals? An audience will laugh at a vain crow, and only later notice that the crow is themselves. A fable lets a storyteller criticise the powerful safely.

2Five fables to know

FableWhat happensMoral
The Tortoise and the HareThe swift hare naps during a race, and the steady tortoise wins.Slow and steady wins the race.
The Ant and the GrasshopperThe ant stores food all summer while the grasshopper sings. In winter the grasshopper goes hungry.Prepare today for the needs of tomorrow.
The Boy Who Cried WolfA shepherd boy lies about a wolf for fun. When a wolf really comes, nobody believes him.Liars are not believed even when they tell the truth.
The Fox and the GrapesA fox cannot reach some grapes and declares they must be sour.It is easy to scorn what you cannot have.
The Lion and the MouseA lion spares a mouse. Later the mouse gnaws through the net that traps the lion.No act of kindness is wasted, and the small can help the great.

"Sour grapes" and "crying wolf" are now everyday English phrases. They are allusions, like the mythological ones in Week 3.

3Fables around the world

The fable belongs to every culture. India has the Panchatantra, animal tales composed more than 2,000 years ago to teach young princes how to rule. West Africa and the Caribbean have the tales of Anansi the spider, a trickster who beats stronger animals with his wits. In France, Jean de La Fontaine turned Aesop into elegant verse in the 1600s.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

State the moral of this fable and give your evidence. "A crow found a piece of cheese and flew to a branch. A fox below said, 'What glossy feathers! Surely your voice is as beautiful. Sing for me.' The crow, delighted, opened her beak to caw. The cheese fell, and the fox ate it."

  1. Problem: the fox wants the cheese but cannot reach the crow.
  2. Method: he praises her, although he cares only about the cheese.
  3. Turning point: "delighted", she opens her beak. Her vanity makes her forget the cheese.
  4. Result: she loses it to the one who praised her.

Answer: Moral: do not trust flatterers. The evidence is that the fox's praise was a tool to get the cheese, and it worked only because the crow enjoyed it.

Hands-on: Write Your Own Fable

You need: paper and pencil

  1. Choose a moral first. Pick a proverb you believe, such as "Look before you leap."
  2. Choose two animals whose natures fit the lesson. Give each exactly one trait.
  3. Plan three beats: a situation, a mistake or clever act, and a result.
  4. Write the fable in no more than 120 words. Put the moral on its own line at the end.
  5. Read it aloud. Then read it again with the moral hidden, and ask your listener to guess it.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. Name three features of a fable.

    Answer and why

    It is short, has animal characters with human traits, and ends with a moral.

    WhyEvery part of a fable serves its single lesson, so nothing extra is included.

  2. What is anthropomorphism?

    Answer and why

    Giving human qualities, speech or behaviour to animals or objects.

    WhyIn fables animals talk, plan and boast like people, so that the tale is really about human behaviour.

  3. What does it mean to say someone's criticism is "sour grapes"?

    Answer and why

    They are pretending to despise something only because they could not get it.

    WhyThe fox calls the grapes sour after failing to reach them, to protect his pride.

  4. Why did the villagers not come when the wolf really appeared?

    Answer and why

    The boy had lied before, so they no longer trusted him.

    WhyTrust, once broken by lying, is not available when it is most needed. That is the moral.

  5. How is a parable different from a fable?

    Answer and why

    A parable uses ordinary human characters and teaches a moral or spiritual truth.

    WhyFables usually use animals and aim at practical wisdom about everyday conduct.

  6. In "The Lion and the Mouse", which two different lessons can a reader draw?

    Answer and why

    Kindness is repaid, and even the weak can help the strong.

    WhyThe lion's mercy is returned, and the tiny mouse does what the mighty lion cannot.

★ Scholar's challenge

Some readers say the ant in "The Ant and the Grasshopper" is wise. Others say the ant is cruel for refusing to share. Rewrite the last two sentences of the fable to teach a different moral, and state that moral.

Answer and why

Example: "The ant opened her door and said, 'Come in and eat. In spring you will teach my children to sing.' Moral: a community needs both the worker and the artist."

WhyChanging the ending shows that the moral is the author's choice, not a fact. The same events can teach thrift or generosity depending on how the story is closed.

Words to own

Fable
A brief story, usually with animal characters, that teaches a lesson.
Moral
The lesson a story teaches.
Anthropomorphism
Giving human characteristics to non-human things.
Parable
A short story about people that teaches a moral or spiritual truth.
Trickster
A clever character who wins by wit instead of strength.
Proverb
A short, well-known saying that states a piece of wisdom.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Is it more effective to tell someone "do not be lazy" or to tell them a story about a grasshopper? Why?
  • Fables give each animal one trait. Is that fair to real foxes, or to real people?
  • Can two good morals contradict each other? Compare "Look before you leap" with "He who hesitates is lost."
