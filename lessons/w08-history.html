🏛 History & Geography · Week 8

The Indus Valley

The big questionFour and a half thousand years ago a city in what is now Pakistan had straight streets, covered drains and a bathroom in almost every house. No palace has been found, no royal tomb, and nobody can read a word its people wrote. How do you study a civilisation that cannot speak?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Locate the Indus Valley civilisation and its main cities.
  2. Describe its urban planning and technology.
  3. Explain what historians can and cannot conclude from the evidence.
  4. Evaluate theories about why the civilisation declined.

1A forgotten giant

The Indus Valley civilisation, also called the Harappan civilisation, flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BC along the Indus River and its neighbours, in today's Pakistan and north-west India. It covered more land than Egypt and Mesopotamia combined, with over a thousand known settlements.

It was forgotten for almost four thousand years. In the 1920s archaeologists excavating at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro realised they had found a third great river civilisation, as old as the other two.

Like Egypt and Mesopotamia, it depended on a river that flooded each year and left fertile silt. Its farmers grew wheat and barley and were among the first people to grow and weave cotton.

2The planned city

Indus cities were not built at random. They were planned.

FeatureEvidence
Grid streetsMain streets run north–south and east–west, crossing at right angles
Standard bricksBaked bricks in the same proportions, 1 : 2 : 4, in cities hundreds of kilometres apart
SanitationHouses with bathing rooms and toilets, linked to covered drains along the streets
Water supplyHundreds of brick-lined wells
Public buildingsThe Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro, a waterproofed pool; large storehouses
Standard weightsStone cubes in a precise system, used throughout the region

No other people of that age built drainage so advanced. Many cities did not match it until the 1800s.

Standard bricks and weights across so wide an area show strong shared rules. Someone was organising, yet we do not know who.

3The silent script

The Indus people carved small stone seals with animals, such as bulls, elephants and a one-horned creature, and with a line of signs. More than 400 different signs are known. Seals were probably pressed into clay to mark ownership of goods.

The script is undeciphered. The inscriptions are very short, about five signs on average, and no "Rosetta Stone" with a known language beside it has been found.

Indus seals and beads have been discovered in Mesopotamia, and Mesopotamian texts mention trade with a land called Meluhha, which was probably the Indus region. The two civilisations traded by sea through the Persian Gulf.

4Reading without words

With no readable texts, historians reason from objects. This is inference, the skill you practised in Literature in Week 1.

EvidenceA reasonable inferenceStill unknown
No palaces or grand royal tombs foundPower may have been shared, or rulers lived modestlyWho governed, and how
Few weapons and no pictures of warLife may have been fairly peacefulWhether they had armies
Houses of similar size, all with drainsLess extreme inequality than in EgyptWhether there were slaves
Uniform bricks and weightsA shared authority or strong shared customsWhether it was one state or many

A careful historian says "the evidence suggests", never "we know".

5The slow ending

By about 1900 BC the great cities were being abandoned, and people moved east into smaller villages. An older theory blamed invaders, but excavation has found no layer of destruction or mass of battle victims. Most scholars now point to environment: the monsoon rains weakened, and rivers shifted course or dried up, including one great river that vanished. Trade with Mesopotamia also faded. The cities were not destroyed. They were left, and many of the people's skills and customs continued in later Indian life.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

An archaeologist finds the same set of stone weights in two Indus cities 800 km apart. What can she infer, and what can she not?

  1. The weights match, so both cities followed one system of measurement.
  2. A shared system makes trade between them fair and simple.
  3. Something must have spread and enforced the standard: a government, a merchants' agreement, or long custom.

Answer: She can infer regular trade and a common standard. She cannot tell from the weights alone who set the standard or whether the cities had one ruler.

Hands-on: Plan Your City and Carve a Seal

You need: squared paper, ruler, coloured pencils, a bar of soap or a slab of clay, a blunt pencil or toothpick

  1. On squared paper lay out a city on a grid: main streets, side lanes, housing blocks, wells, a public bath and a storehouse.
  2. Draw your drainage system in blue. Where does the waste water go? Which way must the ground slope?
  3. Decide one standard brick size and one standard weight. Write the rule that all builders and traders must follow.
  4. Carve a seal in soap or clay: one animal and four signs of your own invention. Remember that a seal prints in mirror image.
  5. Give your city plan and seal to a parent. What can they infer about your city's values without being told anything?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. Where and when did the Indus Valley civilisation flourish?

    Answer and why

    Along the Indus River, in modern Pakistan and north-west India, from about 2600 to 1900 BC.

    WhyIt grew on the fertile floodplain of the Indus and its neighbouring rivers.

  2. Name its two best-known cities.

    Answer and why

    Harappa and Mohenjo-daro

    WhyThese were the first to be excavated, in the 1920s. Harappa gives the civilisation its other name.

  3. Give two pieces of evidence that Indus cities were planned.

    Answer and why

    Grid streets, standard bricks, and covered drains (any two).

    WhyFeatures that repeat across a whole city, and between cities, cannot have arisen by chance.

  4. Why has the Indus script not been deciphered?

    Answer and why

    The inscriptions are very short, and none appears beside a known language.

    WhyDecipherment needs either long texts to reveal patterns or a bilingual text like the Rosetta Stone.

  5. What evidence shows trade between the Indus Valley and Mesopotamia?

    Answer and why

    Indus seals and beads found in Mesopotamia, and Mesopotamian records of a land called Meluhha.

    WhyObjects found far from where they were made are direct evidence of exchange.

  6. What do most scholars now believe caused the decline of the Indus cities?

    Answer and why

    Environmental change: weaker monsoon rains and rivers shifting or drying up.

    WhyThere is little evidence of violent destruction, and people appear to have moved away gradually.

★ Scholar's challenge

Egypt left giant pyramids and statues of kings. The Indus Valley left drains, wells and standard bricks. Write a paragraph on what each civilisation's remains suggest about what its people valued, and explain why you must be cautious.

Answer and why

Egypt's monuments suggest it valued the power and afterlife of its rulers. The Indus remains suggest a concern for order, cleanliness and everyday life.

WhyA strong answer uses specific evidence for each claim, then adds the caution: we can read Egypt's own explanations but not the Indus people's, so our view of them rests on inference, and what survives may not be what mattered most to them.

Words to own

Harappan
Another name for the Indus Valley civilisation.
Urban planning
Designing the layout of a city in advance.
Sanitation
Systems that keep a place clean, especially drains and sewers.
Seal
A carved stamp used to mark ownership.
Undeciphered
Not yet able to be read.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind that brings heavy summer rain to South Asia.

  • Is it possible for a large civilisation to work without kings? What would it need instead?
