Chemical vs Physical Change

The big questionTear a sheet of paper in half and you still have paper. Burn it and you have ash and smoke, and no power on Earth will turn them back. What is the real difference between these two changes?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Distinguish physical changes from chemical changes.
  2. List the signs that a chemical reaction may have taken place.
  3. Name the reactants and products of a reaction and write a word equation.
  4. State and apply the law of conservation of mass.

1Two kinds of change

In a physical change the substance stays the same substance. Only its form changes: its shape, size or state. The particles are rearranged but are not altered. Melting, freezing, boiling, tearing, crushing and dissolving are physical changes, and many can be reversed.

In a chemical change, also called a chemical reaction, bonds between atoms break and new bonds form. One or more new substances appear, with new properties. Burning, rusting, cooking, digesting and rotting are chemical changes. They are usually hard to reverse.

Physical changeChemical change
New substance?NoYes
What changesForm, state, shapeThe bonds between atoms
Reversible?UsuallyUsually not
ExamplesIce melting, sugar dissolvingWood burning, iron rusting

2Signs of a chemical change

  • A gas is produced (bubbles or fizzing).
  • The colour changes permanently.
  • Heat or light is given out, or the mixture turns cold.
  • A solid forms in a liquid. This solid is called a precipitate.
  • A new smell appears.

These are clues, not proof. Boiling water bubbles, but it is a physical change, because the bubbles are still water. The one sure test is the question: has a new substance been made?

Dissolving is the classic trap. Sugar seems to vanish in tea, but the tea is sweet, and if the water evaporates the sugar is still there. No new substance was made, so dissolving is physical.

3Reactants and products

The starting substances are the reactants. The new substances are the products. A word equation records the reaction, with an arrow meaning "react to form".

ReactionWord equation
Rustingiron + oxygen → iron oxide
Burning natural gasmethane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Vinegar and baking sodaacid + sodium bicarbonate → a salt + water + carbon dioxide
Photosynthesiscarbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

4Nothing is lost

In the 1780s the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier weighed everything before and after reactions in sealed containers, including the gases. He found that the total mass never changed. This is the law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, matter is neither created nor destroyed. The atoms are only rearranged.

When wood burns, the ash weighs less than the log. Mass was not destroyed. It left as carbon dioxide and water vapour. Catch those gases, and the totals match.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

12 g of carbon burns completely in 32 g of oxygen. What mass of carbon dioxide is produced?

  1. Word equation: carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide.
  2. Mass is conserved, so the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants.
  3. 12 + 32 = 44.

Answer: 44 g of carbon dioxide

Example 2

Is frying an egg a physical or a chemical change?

  1. Has the form changed? Yes: from a runny liquid to a solid.
  2. Can it be reversed by cooling? No.
  3. Is there a new substance? Yes: the proteins have changed permanently, with new colour, texture and smell.

Answer: A chemical change

Hands-on: The Balloon Reaction

You need: a small plastic bottle, vinegar, baking soda, a balloon, a funnel, a kitchen scale, ice cubes, a saucer

  1. Put an ice cube on the saucer and let it melt. Could you turn it back? Name the kind of change.
  2. Pour 100 mL of vinegar into the bottle. Use the funnel to put two teaspoons of baking soda inside the balloon.
  3. Stretch the balloon over the bottle's neck without spilling the powder. Weigh the whole apparatus and record the mass.
  4. Lift the balloon so the powder falls in. Record every sign of change you observe.
  5. When the fizzing stops, weigh it again with the balloon still attached. Compare the two masses and explain what you find.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. Is dissolving salt in water a physical or a chemical change? How could you show it?

    Answer and why

    Physical. Evaporate the water and the salt is left behind.

    WhyNo new substance forms. The salt particles are only spread out among the water particles.

  2. Name three signs that a chemical change may have happened.

    Answer and why

    A gas forms, the colour changes, heat or light is given out, a precipitate forms, or a new smell appears (any three).

    WhyEach can show that new substances with new properties have been made.

  3. Water bubbles when it boils. Why is this not a chemical change?

    Answer and why

    The bubbles are water vapour, still the same substance.

    WhyOnly the state has changed. Cool the vapour and it becomes liquid water again.

  4. In the reaction iron + oxygen → iron oxide, name the reactants and the product.

    Answer and why

    Reactants: iron and oxygen. Product: iron oxide.

    WhyReactants are written before the arrow and products after it.

  5. 50 g of reactants are sealed in a jar and react completely. What is the mass of the contents afterwards?

    Answer and why

    50 g

    WhyBy the law of conservation of mass, atoms are rearranged but none are created or destroyed.

  6. A candle weighs less after burning for an hour. Does this break the law of conservation of mass?

    Answer and why

    No. The missing mass left as carbon dioxide and water vapour.

    WhyWax reacts with oxygen to form gases that drift away. In a sealed container the total mass would be unchanged.

★ Scholar's challenge

An iron nail is left outside and rusts. After a month it weighs more than it did when new. Explain how a nail can gain mass, and say where the extra mass came from.

Answer and why

The iron combined with oxygen from the air, and the oxygen atoms added their mass.

WhyRusting is iron + oxygen → iron oxide. The product contains all the original iron atoms plus the oxygen atoms that bonded to them. Mass is conserved because the air lost exactly what the nail gained.

Words to own

Physical change
A change in form or state that makes no new substance.
Chemical change
A change that produces one or more new substances.
Reactant
A substance present at the start of a reaction.
Product
A substance formed by a reaction.
Precipitate
A solid that forms within a liquid during a reaction.
Conservation of mass
The law that total mass is unchanged by a chemical reaction.
