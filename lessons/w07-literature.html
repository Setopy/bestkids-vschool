✒ Literature · Week 7

Protagonists

The big questionA good author never writes "Mara was brave." The author shows Mara's hands shaking as she opens the door anyway. Why is the second version so much stronger, and how do writers make people out of ink?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Identify the protagonist and antagonist of a story.
  2. Distinguish direct from indirect characterisation.
  3. Analyse a character through speech, thoughts, effect on others, actions and looks.
  4. Describe a character's motivation, flaw and arc.

1Who the story is about

The protagonist is the central character, the one whose problem drives the story and whose choices we follow. The word is Greek: protos (first) and agonistes (contestant, actor). The antagonist is the force that stands against the protagonist. It may be a person, a society, nature, or something inside the protagonist.

The protagonist is not always good, and the antagonist is not always evil. "Protagonist" means central, not virtuous.

2Telling and showing

An author builds a character in two ways.

Direct characterisation tells you the trait: "Tomás was generous."

Indirect characterisation shows evidence and trusts you to infer the trait: "Tomás broke his bread in two and held out the larger piece."

Good readers gather the evidence with five questions, remembered as STEAL.

LetterAskExample
SpeechWhat does the character say, and how?"I suppose I could help," she muttered.
ThoughtsWhat does the character think and feel in private?He counted the exits.
Effect on othersHow do other characters react?The room fell silent when she entered.
ActionsWhat does the character do?He returned the wallet without opening it.
LooksWhat do appearance and manner suggest?Her boots were patched but polished.

Actions weigh the most. When a character's words and actions disagree, believe the actions.

3Depth and change

TermMeaning
Flat characterBuilt on one trait; simple
Round characterHas several traits, some in conflict; feels real
Static characterStays the same from beginning to end
Dynamic characterChanges in an important way

A motivation is what a character wants and why. A flaw is a weakness that causes trouble. The path of change from the beginning to the end is the character arc. In most strong stories the protagonist is round and dynamic: they want something, their flaw stands in the way, and the events of the plot force them to change.

To describe an arc, complete this sentence: "At first ___ is ___, but after ___, they become ___."

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Analyse this passage with STEAL. "Old Bao arrived at the school gate an hour before the bell, as he had every day for thirty years. He straightened the crooked sign, though nobody had asked him to. When the new headmaster hurried past without a word, Bao only bowed. 'Fine morning,' he said to the empty yard. The smallest children ran to him first."

  1. Speech: "Fine morning", said to an empty yard. He is cheerful and expects no reply.
  2. Thoughts: none are given. We must infer everything.
  3. Effect on others: the smallest children run to him first. He is trusted and loved by those who judge by kindness.
  4. Actions: he comes an hour early for thirty years, straightens the sign unasked, and bows to rudeness. He is dutiful, takes pride in his work, and is patient.
  5. Looks: "Old" is the only detail. The author relies on action.

Answer: Bao is loyal, humble and kind. The author never states any of these traits. Every one is shown.

Hands-on: Character Dossier

You need: a novel you are reading, a notebook, pencils

  1. Name the protagonist and the antagonist. Is the antagonist a person, a group, nature or an inner struggle?
  2. Rule a page into five boxes for STEAL. Find one quotation for each, with its page number.
  3. From your evidence write three trait words. Underline the evidence for each.
  4. Write the protagonist's motivation in one sentence and their flaw in another.
  5. Draw the arc as a line from the first chapter to where you are now. Mark the events that changed the character.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is a protagonist?

    Answer and why

    The central character, whose problem and choices drive the story.

    WhyThe plot is built around what this character wants and what stands in the way.

  2. Must the antagonist be a villain? Explain.

    Answer and why

    No. It is whatever opposes the protagonist, and may be nature, society or an inner conflict.

    WhyA storm, an unjust law, or the character's own fear can all block the goal.

  3. Is this direct or indirect characterisation? "Lucía counted her coins twice, then pushed them all across the counter toward the old man."

    Answer and why

    Indirect

    WhyThe author shows an action and leaves us to infer that Lucía is generous, or self-sacrificing.

  4. What does STEAL stand for?

    Answer and why

    Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks

    WhyThese are the five kinds of evidence an author uses to reveal character indirectly.

  5. What is the difference between a static and a dynamic character?

    Answer and why

    A static character does not change. A dynamic character changes in an important way.

    WhyThe change is usually in belief, attitude or understanding, caused by the events of the story.

  6. A character says, "I am not afraid," while backing toward the door. What should the reader conclude, and why?

    Answer and why

    The character is afraid.

    WhyActions are stronger evidence than words. When the two conflict, the action reveals the truth.

★ Scholar's challenge

Rewrite each "telling" sentence as "showing", in two or three sentences, without using the trait word: (a) "Sam was nervous." (b) "The queen was cruel." (c) "Ada was curious."

Answer and why

Example for (a): "Sam checked his watch, then checked it again. His lines, which he had known perfectly at breakfast, had left his head."

WhyA successful answer gives concrete evidence from STEAL, such as an action or speech, from which a reader could infer the exact trait. Test it by asking someone to name the trait.

Words to own

Protagonist
The main character of a story.
Antagonist
The character or force opposing the protagonist.
Characterisation
The methods an author uses to reveal character.
Motivation
The reason behind a character's actions.
Dynamic character
A character who changes during the story.
Character arc
The path of a character's change across a story.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Can a story have a protagonist you dislike? Would you keep reading?
  • Why do readers believe a character's actions more than the narrator's descriptions?
  • Think of yourself as the protagonist of this year. What is your motivation, and what is your flaw?
