⚛ Science · Week 9

Experiment Design

The big questionA boy waters one plant with cola and one with water. The cola plant dies and he declares that cola kills plants. But the cola plant was also in a darker corner, in a smaller pot, and was a different kind of plant. What has he actually proved?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Write a testable question and a hypothesis.
  2. Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  3. Design a fair test with repeated trials and a control.
  4. Record data in a table, find a mean, and write an honest conclusion.

1How science decides

Science is a method for finding out what is true by testing ideas against the world. Its steps form a cycle.

  • Observe something and ask a question.
  • Form a hypothesis: a testable prediction with a reason.
  • Design and carry out an experiment.
  • Record and analyse the data.
  • Draw a conclusion, and ask a new question.

A good scientific question can be answered by measuring. "Which paper towel is best?" is vague. "Which brand of paper towel absorbs the most water in ten seconds?" can be tested.

A hypothesis is often written: If (I change this), then (this will happen), because (reason). A hypothesis that turns out wrong is not a failure. It is a result.

2Variables

A variable is anything that can change in an experiment.

TypeMeaningHow many
Independent variableThe one thing you change on purposeOne
Dependent variableWhat you measure to see the effectOne or more
Controlled variablesEverything you keep the sameAll the rest

A fair test changes only one variable. If two things change at once you cannot tell which caused the result. That was the boy's mistake: liquid, light, pot size and species all differed.

A control is a set-up with no treatment, used for comparison. To test a fertiliser you need plants with none.

3Reliable results

  • Repeat. One result may be a fluke. Run at least three trials for each condition.
  • Average. Add the trials and divide by how many there are. This is the mean.
  • Watch for anomalies. An anomalous result is one far from the others. Investigate it. Never hide it.
  • Measure precisely, with suitable instruments and SI units from Week 1.
  • Keep safe. Identify hazards before you begin.

4Recording and concluding

Put the independent variable in the first column of the table and the dependent variable, with its unit, in the next columns. On a graph, the independent variable goes along the bottom (the x-axis) and the dependent variable up the side (the y-axis).

A conclusion must do four things: state what happened, quote the data, say whether the hypothesis was supported, and mention at least one limitation or source of error. Scientists write "the data support the hypothesis". They do not write "I proved it", because later evidence may change the picture.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Design a fair test for the question "Does water temperature affect how fast sugar dissolves?"

  1. Hypothesis: if the water is hotter, the sugar will dissolve faster, because faster-moving water particles strike the sugar more often and harder.
  2. Independent variable: water temperature (10 °C, 30 °C, 50 °C).
  3. Dependent variable: time for the sugar to disappear, in seconds.
  4. Controlled variables: volume of water, mass and type of sugar, the same glass, no stirring or the same stirring.
  5. Run three trials at each temperature and find the mean.

Answer: One variable is changed, one is measured, the rest are held constant, and every condition is repeated three times.

Example 2

The three trials at 30 °C gave 42 s, 45 s and 39 s. Find the mean.

  1. Add the results: 42 + 45 + 39 = 126.
  2. Divide by the number of trials: 126 ÷ 3.

Answer: The mean time is 42 s.

Hands-on: Your First Investigation: the Paper Helicopter

You need: paper, scissors, paper clips, a ruler, a stopwatch, a safe high place from which to drop (an adult helps)

  1. Cut a strip 20 cm by 5 cm. Cut 8 cm down the middle from one end to make two blades, and fold them opposite ways. Add one paper clip to the bottom.
  2. Choose one question: does blade length, the number of paper clips, or the drop height change the time to fall?
  3. Write your hypothesis as "If … then … because …". List your independent, dependent and controlled variables.
  4. Test at least three values of your independent variable, with three trials each. Record everything in a table and calculate each mean.
  5. Draw a graph. Write a four-part conclusion. What would you improve if you repeated it?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is a hypothesis?

    Answer and why

    A testable prediction, with a reason, about what will happen.

    WhyIt turns a question into something an experiment can support or contradict.

  2. In an experiment on whether the amount of light affects how tall a plant grows, name the independent and dependent variables.

    Answer and why

    Independent: the amount of light. Dependent: the height of the plant.

    WhyThe independent variable is what you change on purpose. The dependent variable is what you measure in response.

  3. Name three variables that should be controlled in that plant experiment.

    Answer and why

    The type of plant, the amount of water, and the soil or pot size (others include temperature).

    WhyIf any of these differed, they might cause the difference in height instead of the light.

  4. Why should an experiment be repeated several times?

    Answer and why

    To make sure the result is reliable and not a fluke.

    WhyRepeated trials reveal the usual result and expose anomalies caused by error or chance.

  5. Four trials gave 18 cm, 22 cm, 20 cm and 20 cm. What is the mean?

    Answer and why

    20 cm

    WhyThe total is 80, and 80 ÷ 4 = 20.

  6. Explain why the boy in the opening question cannot conclude that cola kills plants.

    Answer and why

    He changed several variables at once, so the test was not fair.

    WhyThe liquid, the light, the pot and the species all differed, and any of them might have caused the plant's death.

★ Scholar's challenge

A company claims: "Nine out of ten people who took our vitamin did not catch a cold this winter." Give three questions a scientist would ask before believing that the vitamin prevents colds.

Answer and why

How many people were tested? Was there a control group that took no vitamin? Were the two groups otherwise alike?

WhyWith only ten people the result could be chance. Without a control we do not know how many would have stayed healthy anyway, and it may also be nine in ten. If the vitamin-takers were younger, fitter or less exposed, the vitamin may not be the cause.

Words to own

Hypothesis
A testable prediction with a reason.
Independent variable
The factor an experimenter deliberately changes.
Dependent variable
The factor that is measured.
Controlled variable
A factor kept the same to make a test fair.
Control
An untreated set-up used for comparison.
Mean
The average: the total divided by the number of values.
