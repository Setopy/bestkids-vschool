✒ Literature · Week 1

Active Reading

The big questionTwo people read the same page. One remembers almost nothing. The other could teach it tomorrow. What did the second reader do differently?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Explain the difference between passive and active reading.
  2. Use six reader's moves: predict, question, clarify, connect, visualise and summarise.
  3. Annotate a passage with a simple code of marks.
  4. Support an idea about a text with evidence from the text.

1Reading is a conversation

A passive reader lets the words pass by, like scenery through a bus window. An active reader talks back to the page: asking, guessing, arguing and checking. The author has put meaning into the text, but the reader must do the work of bringing it out.

Great readers are not faster. They are more awake.

2The six moves

MoveWhat you doWhat you might say
PredictGuess what comes next"I think she will open the door because…"
QuestionAsk the text something"Why did he lie?"
ClarifyStop at confusion and fix it"I do not know this word. Let me reread."
ConnectLink to your life, another book, or the world"This is like when…"
VisualiseBuild the picture in your mind"I can see the dust on the road."
SummariseSay the main idea in your own words"So far, in one sentence…"

3Annotation: thinking on paper

To annotate is to mark a text with your thoughts. Use a pencil and a small code:

  • Underline one key sentence per paragraph, never more.
  • ? in the margin beside anything puzzling.
  • ! beside anything surprising or important.
  • Circle words you do not know, then look them up.
  • → beside a place where you made a prediction or connection, with two or three words of note.

4Evidence

A strong reader never says only "I think". A strong reader says "I think this, because the text says that." The words you point to are your evidence. A conclusion you reach from clues is an inference.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Read actively: "Amara stood at the edge of the market long after the stalls had closed. In her pocket the three coins felt lighter than they had that morning. She did not look toward the baker's shuttered window. She looked at her shoes."

  1. Question: why did she stay after the stalls closed?
  2. Clarify: "shuttered" means closed with shutters.
  3. Infer: the coins "felt lighter", yet coins do not change weight. They now seem worth less to her, so something she hoped to buy cost more than she had.
  4. Evidence: she avoids looking at the baker's window, and she looks down at her shoes.
  5. Summarise: Amara could not afford what she wanted from the baker and feels ashamed or sad.

Answer: The passage never says "Amara was disappointed", but an active reader can prove it from the coins, the window and the shoes.

Hands-on: The Margin Conversation

You need: a short story or chapter you have not read, a pencil, sticky notes if the book must stay clean

  1. Before reading, look only at the title and first sentence. Write one prediction.
  2. Read the first page with your pencil in your hand. Make at least one mark of each kind from the code.
  3. Stop at the end of the page. Without looking, say a one-sentence summary aloud.
  4. Check your prediction. Were you right? It does not matter. What matters is that you were paying attention.
  5. Tell a parent one inference and point to the exact words that are your evidence.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is the difference between passive and active reading?

    Answer and why

    A passive reader only receives words. An active reader questions, predicts and checks while reading.

    WhyUnderstanding is built by the reader's own thinking, so a reader who does nothing with the words keeps very little of them.

  2. You reach a sentence you do not understand. Which move do you use, and what do you do?

    Answer and why

    Clarify: stop, reread, and look up or work out the hard word.

    WhyReading on while confused piles new confusion on old. Fixing it at once keeps the meaning whole.

  3. "The dog's tail dropped and it crept under the table when the thunder began." What can you infer? Give your evidence.

    Answer and why

    The dog is afraid of thunder.

    WhyA dropped tail and hiding are signs of fear, and both happen exactly "when the thunder began". The text never uses the word afraid.

  4. Why should you underline only one sentence in each paragraph?

    Answer and why

    Because underlining forces you to choose what matters most.

    WhyIf everything is marked, nothing stands out. Choosing is itself an act of thinking.

  5. Write a prediction for a story that opens: "Nobody had opened the blue door since Grandfather left."

    Answer and why

    Any reasoned prediction, for example: "Someone will open the door and learn why Grandfather left."

    WhyA good prediction grows from clues. Here the author points at the door and at Grandfather's leaving, so both are likely to matter.

  6. What is evidence, in reading?

    Answer and why

    The exact words in the text that support your idea.

    WhyEvidence lets another reader check your thinking. Without it an idea is only an opinion.

★ Scholar's challenge

Reread the Amara passage. Write two different inferences about why the coins "felt lighter", each supported by evidence. Then decide which is stronger and why.

Answer and why

Example: (1) prices rose and she cannot afford bread, supported by her avoiding the baker's window; (2) she lost or spent some coins, supported by "lighter than that morning".

WhyInference 1 is stronger because the text says there are still three coins, and it gives the baker detail a purpose. The best inference explains the most clues.

Words to own

Annotate
To add notes and marks to a text while reading.
Inference
A conclusion reached from clues, not stated directly.
Evidence
Words from the text that support an idea.
Summarise
To restate the main idea briefly in your own words.
Predict
To make a reasoned guess about what will happen.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Is it possible to read a book "wrongly"? Who decides what a story means, the author or the reader?
  • Why might two careful readers reach different inferences from the same passage?
  • Which of the six moves do you already use without noticing? Which is hardest for you?
