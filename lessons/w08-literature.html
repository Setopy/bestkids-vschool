Setting as Character

The big questionMove a story from a sunlit beach to a locked house in a snowstorm and you have a different story, even if every character stays the same. Is the setting only a background, or can a place act on a story the way a person does?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Identify the four parts of a setting.
  2. Explain how setting creates mood and conflict.
  3. Recognise personification and sensory detail in descriptions of place.
  4. Write a setting that affects the characters within it.

1More than a place

Setting is where and when a story happens. It has four parts.

PartQuestions
PlaceWhich country, landscape, building, room?
TimeWhich era, season, hour?
Social environmentWhat are the customs, rules and ways of life?
AtmosphereWhat is the weather, the light, the feeling in the air?

A weak writer names the setting and moves on. A strong writer makes the setting do something.

2Four jobs a setting can do

  • It creates mood. Mood is the feeling a passage produces in the reader. "Fog pressed against the windows" produces unease before anything has happened.
  • It creates conflict. A desert, a storm or a frozen sea can be the antagonist. This is the conflict of a person against nature.
  • It reveals character. A bedroom, a desk or a garden tells us about its owner. A character's reaction to a place tells us even more.
  • It carries meaning. A place can be a symbol, standing for an idea. A walled garden may stand for safety, or for a prison.

When a setting shapes events, resists the characters and seems to have a will of its own, we say it works like a character.

3The writer's tools

Sensory detail. Use all five senses, not only sight. What does the place sound, smell and feel like?

Personification. Give the place human actions or feelings: "The old house groaned." "The city never slept." "The forest swallowed the path."

Precise verbs. Wind can whisper, moan, shriek or bully. The verb sets the mood.

Selection. Do not list everything. Choose the two or three details that produce the feeling you want.

When the weather or landscape matches a character's emotions, such as rain at a funeral or sunshine at a reunion, writers call it the pathetic fallacy. Here "pathetic" means "to do with feeling".

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Compare two descriptions of the same forest. (A) "Sunlight fell in coins through the leaves. Somewhere a stream was laughing, and the path ran ahead like a friendly dog." (B) "The trees stood close, shoulder to shoulder, and let no light through. The path had given up. Something dripped."

  1. Both describe trees, light and a path. The facts are nearly the same.
  2. A uses "coins", "laughing" and "friendly dog": bright images and playful personification. The mood is welcoming.
  3. B personifies the trees as a silent crowd ("shoulder to shoulder", "let no light through") and the path as defeated ("had given up"). The unexplained sound "Something dripped" adds threat.
  4. In B the forest acts against a traveller. It blocks, hides and refuses.

Answer: The same place produces opposite moods through the writer's choice of details, verbs and personification. In B the setting behaves like an antagonist.

Hands-on: One Room, Three Moods

You need: notebook, pencil, a room in your home

  1. Sit in one room for five minutes. List what you notice with each of the five senses.
  2. Write four sentences describing the room so that it feels safe and happy. You may not use the words safe, happy or nice.
  3. Describe the same room in four sentences so that it feels frightening. Change no facts, only the details and verbs you choose.
  4. Describe it a third time as sad or lonely.
  5. Read all three aloud without saying which is which. Can your listener name each mood and point to the words that created it?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What are the four parts of a setting?

    Answer and why

    Place, time, social environment and atmosphere

    WhyTogether they answer where, when, among what customs, and in what conditions the story happens.

  2. What is mood?

    Answer and why

    The feeling a piece of writing creates in the reader.

    WhyAuthors build it through their choice of details, images and verbs, often in descriptions of setting.

  3. Identify the personification: "The wind shouldered the door open and wandered through the hall."

    Answer and why

    "shouldered" and "wandered"

    WhyBoth give the wind human actions, making it seem like an uninvited visitor.

  4. How can a setting create conflict? Give an example.

    Answer and why

    By standing in the way of what a character wants, such as a blizzard trapping travellers on a mountain.

    WhyWhen nature or place opposes the protagonist, it plays the role of antagonist.

  5. What is the pathetic fallacy?

    Answer and why

    Making the weather or landscape reflect a character's feelings.

    WhyA storm during an argument, for example, strengthens the mood by matching the outer world to the inner one.

  6. A story describes a boy's room: "maps on every wall, a telescope at the window, a suitcase never fully unpacked." What does the setting reveal about him?

    Answer and why

    He longs to travel or explore, and does not feel settled.

    WhyMaps and a telescope look outward, and the half-packed suitcase suggests someone ready to leave.

★ Scholar's challenge

Write a paragraph of six to eight sentences in which a place is the antagonist. A character must want something, and the setting must actively prevent it. Use at least two examples of personification and three different senses. No other characters may appear.

Answer and why

Answers will vary.

WhyCheck four things: the character has a clear goal; the place blocks it through its own "actions"; personification and three senses are present; and the mood stays the same from the first sentence to the last.

Words to own

Setting
The time, place and conditions in which a story happens.
Mood
The feeling a text creates in the reader.
Atmosphere
The weather, light and emotional air of a scene.
Personification
Giving human qualities to something that is not human.
Sensory detail
Description that appeals to sight, sound, smell, taste or touch.
Symbol
Something that stands for an idea beyond itself.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Could your favourite story happen anywhere else? What would be lost?
  • Does where you grow up shape who you become, the way a setting shapes a character?
  • Why do so many frightening stories take place at night? Could you write a frightening scene at noon on a sunny day?
