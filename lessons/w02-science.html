⚛ Science · Week 2

Particle Theory

The big questionIce, water and steam are the same substance. If nothing is added or taken away, what actually changes when ice melts?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. State the main ideas of the particle theory of matter.
  2. Describe how particles are arranged and move in solids, liquids and gases.
  3. Explain melting, boiling, condensing, freezing and sublimation in terms of particles and energy.
  4. Use the theory to explain diffusion and expansion.

1The four big ideas

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. The particle theory says:

  • All matter is made of tiny particles, far too small to see.
  • The particles are always moving.
  • There are forces of attraction between particles.
  • When matter is heated, its particles gain energy and move faster.

Temperature is a measure of how fast the particles are moving, on average.

2Three states

SolidLiquidGas
ArrangementClose together in a regular patternClose together, no patternFar apart
MovementVibrate in placeSlide past each otherFly fast in all directions
ShapeFixedTakes the shape of its containerFills its container
VolumeFixedFixedChanges; can be squeezed
AttractionStrongMediumVery weak

A gas can be compressed because there is empty space between its particles. Solids and liquids cannot easily be compressed because their particles already touch.

3Changing state

Heating gives particles energy. In a solid they vibrate harder until they break out of their fixed places: the solid melts. Heat the liquid further and particles escape completely: it boils into a gas. Cooling reverses each step: gas condenses to liquid, and liquid freezes to solid. A few solids, such as dry ice, turn straight into gas. This is sublimation.

During a change of state the temperature stays the same. Melting ice stays at 0 °C until the last crystal is gone, because the energy is being used to pull particles apart, not to speed them up.

The particles themselves never change. Water particles in ice, water and steam are identical. Only their spacing and speed differ, so the mass stays the same.

4Two things the theory explains

Diffusion is the spreading of particles from where there are many to where there are few. It happens because particles move randomly. It is why you can smell cooking from another room.

Thermal expansion happens because faster particles push each other further apart. The particles do not grow. This is why bridges have expansion gaps and why a tight metal lid loosens under hot water.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Explain, using particles, why a puddle disappears on a warm day even though it never reaches 100 °C.

  1. Particles in a liquid move at many different speeds.
  2. Some of the fastest ones at the surface have enough energy to escape as gas.
  3. This is evaporation. It happens at any temperature, only from the surface.
  4. Warmth gives more particles enough energy, so the puddle dries faster.

Answer: The fastest surface particles escape one by one. Boiling is not required.

Hands-on: The Race of the Drops

You need: two clear glasses, hot and cold water, food colouring, a balloon, an empty plastic bottle, a bowl of hot water

  1. Fill one glass with cold water and one with hot. Let them stand until still.
  2. Add one drop of colouring to each at the same moment. Do not stir. Sketch each glass after 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 5 minutes.
  3. Explain the difference using the word diffusion and the speed of particles.
  4. Stretch the balloon over the mouth of the empty bottle. Stand the bottle in the bowl of hot water and watch.
  5. Explain what happened to the air particles. Did their number change?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. Why can you squeeze air in a syringe but not water?

    Answer and why

    Gas particles are far apart, with space to be pushed into. Liquid particles already touch.

    WhyCompression needs empty space between particles, and only gases have much of it.

  2. What happens to the particles of a solid when it melts?

    Answer and why

    They gain enough energy to break out of their fixed positions and slide past each other.

    WhyHeating makes them vibrate harder until the attraction can no longer hold the regular pattern.

  3. 50 g of ice melts completely. What is the mass of the water?

    Answer and why

    50 g

    WhyNo particles were added or removed. Only their arrangement changed.

  4. Why does the temperature of boiling water stay at 100 °C however strongly you heat it?

    Answer and why

    The extra energy is used to separate particles into gas, not to make them faster.

    WhyTemperature measures particle speed. During a change of state the energy goes into overcoming attraction.

  5. A metal railway track is longer in summer than in winter. Did its particles get bigger?

    Answer and why

    No. They moved further apart.

    WhyHotter particles vibrate more widely and push their neighbours away. The particles themselves are unchanged.

  6. Why does a smell spread faster in a warm room than a cold one?

    Answer and why

    Particles move faster at higher temperature, so they diffuse faster.

    WhyDiffusion is caused by random particle motion, and temperature is a measure of the speed of that motion.

★ Scholar's challenge

On a cold morning, drops of water appear on the outside of a glass of iced juice. The glass is not leaking. Where did the water come from, and why did it appear there?

Answer and why

From water vapour in the air, which condensed on the cold glass.

WhyAir contains invisible water gas. Particles that hit the cold surface lose energy, slow down and are pulled together into liquid drops.

Words to own

Matter
Anything that has mass and takes up space.
Particle
A tiny piece of matter, such as an atom or molecule.
Diffusion
The spreading of particles from a crowded region to a less crowded one.
Sublimation
A change directly from solid to gas.
Condensation
A change from gas to liquid.
Evaporation
A change from liquid to gas at the surface, below the boiling point.
