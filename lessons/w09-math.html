∑ Mathematics · Week 9

Q1 Synthesis

The big questionFor eight weeks you have collected tools: bases, primes, the order of operations, bar models, deduction, sequences and variables. A real problem never tells you which tool to pick up. How does a mathematician decide where to begin?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Choose a suitable strategy for an unfamiliar problem.
  2. Combine two or more Quarter 1 skills in a single solution.
  3. Follow Pólya's four steps and write a complete, checked solution.
  4. Identify your own strengths and gaps before Quarter 2.

1The toolbox

WeekToolUse it when
1Bases and place valueA number is written in an unusual system, or grouping matters
2Divisibility, primes, GCF, LCMQuestions about factors, sharing equally, or events repeating together
3Order of operationsAn expression has several operations
4–5Bar modelsA word problem compares quantities or describes a change
6Deduction by casesDigits or values are hidden and rules restrict them
7Sequences and nth termsSomething grows by a pattern
8Variables and equationsAn unknown must be named and found

Most real problems need two tools at once. Often a bar model and an equation are the same solution written in two languages.

2Pólya's four steps

The Hungarian mathematician George Pólya described how good problem-solvers think in his 1945 book How to Solve It.

  • Understand. What is known? What is unknown? What are the conditions? Say the problem in your own words.
  • Plan. Have you seen something like it? Can you draw it, name a variable, look for a pattern, try a simpler case, or work backwards?
  • Carry out. Work carefully, one line at a time, with a reason for each step.
  • Look back. Check the answer against the original problem. Is it sensible? Is there another way? What did you learn?

Most pupils skip the first and last steps. Scholars do not.

3When you are stuck

Being stuck is a normal part of mathematics, not a sign of failure. Try these in order: reread the question slowly; draw a diagram; make the numbers smaller; list what you know; ask what the answer would have to look like; leave the problem and come back to it.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

In base five, the number 3▢4 equals 99 in base ten. Find the missing digit.

  1. Understand: the columns in base five are 25, 5 and 1. The missing digit sits in the fives column.
  2. Plan: name it x and write an equation from place value.
  3. Carry out: 3 × 25 + 5x + 4 = 99, so 79 + 5x = 99, so 5x = 20 and x = 4.
  4. Look back: 4 is an allowed digit in base five. 344₅ = 75 + 20 + 4 = 99. ✓

Answer: The missing digit is 4. (Tools: Week 1 and Week 8.)

Example 2

Lia thinks of a number, multiplies it by 3 and subtracts 7. The result is a prime between 20 and 30 that is 2 more than a multiple of 9. What was her number?

  1. The primes between 20 and 30 are 23 and 29.
  2. Multiples of 9 near them: 18 and 27. Two more gives 20 or 29. So the result is 29.
  3. Let her number be n: 3n − 7 = 29, so 3n = 36 and n = 12.
  4. Look back: 3 × 12 − 7 = 29, which is prime and equals 27 + 2. ✓

Answer: Her number was 12. (Tools: Week 2 and Week 8.)

Example 3

Solve the cryptarithm AB + BA = CAC.

  1. Two two-digit numbers add to less than 200, so C = 1.
  2. By place value, AB + BA = 10A + B + 10B + A = 11 × (A + B). So CAC is a multiple of 11.
  3. CAC is 1A1. Its value is 101 + 10A, and it must equal 11 × (A + B).
  4. The three-digit multiples of 11 of the form 1A1 are 121 only (11 × 11). So A = 2 and A + B = 11, giving B = 9.
  5. Look back: 29 + 92 = 121. ✓

Answer: A = 2, B = 9, C = 1. (Tools: Weeks 1, 2 and 6.)

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. Convert 77 to base two.

    Answer and why

    1001101₂

    Why77 = 64 + 8 + 4 + 1, so put 1 in those columns and 0 in the 32, 16 and 2 columns.

  2. Find the prime factorisation of 420, and the GCF of 420 and 126.

    Answer and why

    420 = 2² × 3 × 5 × 7; the GCF is 42

    Why126 = 2 × 3² × 7. The shared primes, each taken the fewest times, are 2 × 3 × 7 = 42.

  3. Evaluate 6 + 3 × (10 − 4)² ÷ 9.

    Answer and why

    18

    WhyBrackets give 6, the exponent gives 36, then 3 × 36 = 108 and 108 ÷ 9 = 12, and finally 6 + 12 = 18.

  4. Ana, Ben and Cal share $156. Ben gets twice as much as Ana, and Cal gets three times as much as Ana. How much does each get?

    Answer and why

    Ana $26, Ben $52, Cal $78

    WhyAna is 1 unit, Ben 2 and Cal 3, making 6 units. 156 ÷ 6 = 26.

  5. A sequence has nth term 7n − 3. Which term is equal to 137?

    Answer and why

    The 20th term

    Why7n − 3 = 137 gives 7n = 140, so n = 20.

  6. Solve 4(x − 2) + 3 = 27.

    Answer and why

    x = 8

    WhySubtract 3: 4(x − 2) = 24. Divide by 4: x − 2 = 6. Add 2: x = 8. Check: 4 × 6 + 3 = 27.

  7. Write the fifth triangular number in base two.

    Answer and why

    1111₂

    WhyThe fifth triangular number is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15, and 15 = 8 + 4 + 2 + 1.

  8. The sum of three consecutive odd numbers is 75. Find them.

    Answer and why

    23, 25 and 27

    WhyCall the middle number m. The three are m − 2, m and m + 2, which add to 3m. So 3m = 75 and m = 25.

  9. Three buses leave a station together at 9:00. They then leave every 8, 12 and 15 minutes. When do all three next leave together?

    Answer and why

    At 11:00

    WhyThe LCM of 8 = 2³, 12 = 2² × 3 and 15 = 3 × 5 is 2³ × 3 × 5 = 120 minutes, which is two hours.

★ Scholar's challenge

The sum 1 + 2 + 3 + … + n equals 231. Find n.

Answer and why

n = 21

WhyThe sum is n × (n + 1) ÷ 2, so n × (n + 1) = 462. We need two consecutive numbers with product 462. Since 462 = 2 × 3 × 7 × 11 = 21 × 22, n = 21. (Tools: Weeks 2, 7 and 8.)
