🏛 History & Geography · Week 6

Mesopotamia

The big questionThe 60-minute hour, the wheel, the written word, the city and the written law all come from one strip of land between two rivers in what is now Iraq. Why there?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Locate Mesopotamia and explain its name.
  2. Describe how Sumerian city-states were organised.
  3. Explain how and why writing was invented.
  4. List Mesopotamian inventions that are still in use.

1The land between the rivers

Mesopotamia is Greek for "between the rivers": meso (middle) and potamos (river). The rivers are the Tigris and the Euphrates, which rise in the mountains of Turkey and flow to the Persian Gulf. The land lies at the eastern end of the Fertile Crescent.

The south, called Sumer, was flat, hot and almost rainless, but the rivers brought rich silt. There was a serious problem: the floods came violently and without warning, and in late spring, when they could destroy the growing crops. To survive, people had to build canals, dykes and reservoirs together. Large projects need organisers, records and rules. Geography pushed Sumer toward government.

2City-states

By about 3500 BC Sumer had real cities, such as Uruk and Ur, each with tens of thousands of people. Each city ruled the farmland around it as an independent city-state, with its own king and its own guardian god.

At the centre stood the ziggurat, a huge stepped tower of mud brick with a temple on top. The temple also served as a storehouse and a bank. Society had clear ranks.

RankWho
TopKing, priests
UpperScribes, officials, merchants
MiddleCraftspeople, farmers
BottomEnslaved people, often war captives or debtors

Sumer had almost no stone, timber or metal, so its merchants traded grain and cloth for them over long distances.

3The invention of writing

Temple officials needed to record how much grain, how many sheep and how much land. At first they used small clay tokens. By about 3300 BC they were pressing signs into wet clay tablets with a cut reed, the stylus. Its tip left wedge-shaped marks, so we call the script cuneiform, from Latin cuneus, a wedge.

Writing began as accounting. Over centuries the signs came to stand for sounds, and then anything that could be said could be written: laws, letters, medicine, mathematics, and stories. Learning the hundreds of signs took years, so trained scribes were powerful and respected.

The greatest Mesopotamian story is the Epic of Gilgamesh, about a king of Uruk who seeks eternal life after his friend's death. It is among the oldest surviving works of literature in the world.

5From cities to empires

The city-states fought each other constantly over land and water. About 2334 BC Sargon of Akkad conquered them all and created the world's first empire: many peoples under one ruler. Later Babylon rose, and its king Hammurabi issued a famous code of laws, which you will study in Week 11.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Use the "chain" from Week 5 to explain why writing appeared in Sumer.

  1. Irrigation farming on rich silt produced a large surplus.
  2. The surplus was stored in temples and had to be counted and shared out.
  3. Memory was not enough for thousands of transactions, so officials made marks on clay.
  4. The surplus also fed full-time scribes, who developed the marks into a complete script.

Answer: Writing was invented to keep accounts of a surplus. Stories and laws came later.

Hands-on: Be a Scribe

You need: air-drying clay or salt dough, a chopstick or cut drinking straw, a rolling pin

  1. Roll the clay into a flat tablet that fits in your palm.
  2. Practise pressing the end of your stylus at an angle to make wedges. Try vertical, horizontal and slanted wedges.
  3. Invent ten signs made only of wedges for things a temple would count: grain, sheep, oxen, jars of oil, and the numbers 1, 10 and 60.
  4. Write a temple record on your tablet, for example "60 sheep, 10 jars of oil".
  5. Let it dry. Ask a parent to read it using your sign list. What made your script easy or hard to read?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What does the name "Mesopotamia" mean?

    Answer and why

    "The land between the rivers"

    WhyIt comes from the Greek meso (middle) and potamos (river), referring to the Tigris and Euphrates.

  2. Why did the farmers of Sumer need to cooperate on a large scale?

    Answer and why

    To build and maintain the canals and dykes that controlled the rivers.

    WhyThe land had little rain and violent floods, so no single family could manage the water alone.

  3. What is a city-state?

    Answer and why

    An independent city that governs itself and the surrounding farmland.

    WhyEach Sumerian city, such as Ur or Uruk, had its own ruler, army and guardian god.

  4. What was writing first used for?

    Answer and why

    Recording goods: grain, animals and land.

    WhyTemple officials needed dependable accounts of what was stored, owed and paid.

  5. Why is the script called cuneiform?

    Answer and why

    Its marks are wedge-shaped.

    WhyThe cut reed stylus left wedges in wet clay, and cuneus is Latin for wedge.

  6. Give two Mesopotamian inventions we still use.

    Answer and why

    The wheel, writing, base-60 time-keeping, the plough or the sail (any two).

    WhyMany basic tools of civilisation were first developed, or first recorded, in Sumer.

★ Scholar's challenge

Mesopotamia had no natural walls. It was open to attack from mountains and deserts on every side. Egypt, as you will see next week, was protected by deserts and sea. Predict two ways in which this difference might have shaped Mesopotamian life and beliefs.

Answer and why

Frequent wars and changes of ruler, walled cities, and a view of the world as uncertain and the gods as hard to please (any two, with reasons).

WhyOpen borders invited invasion, so cities built walls and empires rose and fell quickly. Together with unpredictable floods, this insecurity is reflected in Mesopotamian stories such as Gilgamesh, which are anxious about death and fate.
