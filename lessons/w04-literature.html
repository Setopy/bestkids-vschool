✒ Literature · Week 4

Norse Myth

The big questionThe Greek gods live for ever. The Norse gods know the exact day they will die and how, and they march out to fight anyway. What kind of people tell stories in which even the gods lose?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Describe the Norse cosmos: Yggdrasil and its worlds.
  2. Name the main Norse gods and their roles.
  3. Retell the binding of Fenrir and explain the idea of Ragnarök.
  4. Compare Norse and Greek myth, and recognise a kenning.

1Stories from the North

Norse myths were told by the peoples of Scandinavia and Iceland, including the Vikings, between about AD 700 and 1100. They were passed on by mouth for centuries. Most of what we know was written down in Iceland in the 1200s in two books: the Poetic Edda, a collection of old poems, and the Prose Edda by the scholar Snorri Sturluson.

Their homeland was cold and dark for much of the year, with dangerous seas. The myths are harder and grimmer than the Greek ones. They are also funny, and they prize courage and loyalty.

2The world tree

At the centre of everything stands Yggdrasil, an immense ash tree. Its branches and roots hold nine worlds.

WorldWho lives there
AsgardThe gods, called the Aesir
MidgardHuman beings ("middle enclosure")
JotunheimThe giants, enemies of the gods
HelThe dead who did not fall in battle

A rainbow bridge, Bifrost, joins Asgard to Midgard. Warriors who die bravely are carried to Valhalla, Odin's hall.

Four English weekdays carry their names: Tuesday (Tyr's day), Wednesday (Woden's, that is Odin's, day), Thursday (Thor's day) and Friday (Frigg's day).

4The binding of Fenrir

The gods learn that the wolf Fenrir, a child of Loki, will one day kill Odin. They raise him in Asgard, but he grows enormous. Twice they bind him with iron chains as a "game", and twice he snaps them. Then the dwarfs make a ribbon called Gleipnir, thin as silk and woven from impossible things, such as the sound of a cat's footstep.

Fenrir suspects a trick. He will allow the ribbon only if a god places a hand in his jaws as a pledge of good faith. Only Tyr steps forward. The ribbon holds. Fenrir is bound, and he bites off Tyr's hand.

The gods are safe for a time. But they won by breaking their word, and Tyr, the god of law, paid the price.

5Ragnarök

Ragnarök is the foretold end. Fenrir will break free, the sea serpent will rise, and gods and giants will destroy each other. Odin will be swallowed by the wolf. Thor will kill the serpent and die from its venom. The world will sink into the sea, and afterwards a new green world will rise.

The gods know all this and cannot prevent it. Their greatness lies in how they meet it. To the Norse, courage meant doing what is right even when you know you will lose.

6Kennings

Norse poets loved the kenning, a two-word riddle that stands in for a plain noun.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Compare how Greek and Norse myths treat fate.

  1. Greek: the gods are immortal. Fate strikes humans, usually as punishment for hubris (Icarus, Arachne).
  2. Norse: fate binds the gods too. Odin gathers wisdom and warriors although he knows he will still fall.
  3. Evidence: Ragnarök is foretold, and the gods prepare for it instead of escaping it.

Answer: In Greek myth the lesson is "know your limits". In Norse myth the lesson is "face your fate with courage".

Hands-on: Kennings and the World Tree

You need: large paper, coloured pencils

  1. Draw Yggdrasil filling the page. Place Asgard, Midgard, Jotunheim and Hel, and add Bifrost.
  2. Invent six kennings for modern things: a car, a phone, a school, rain, a library, a football.
  3. Read them to a parent as riddles. Can they guess each one?
  4. Write four lines describing a storm using at least two kennings.
  5. Find out which god or thing Saturday, Sunday and Monday are named after. Are they Norse?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. In which two books were the Norse myths written down?

    Answer and why

    The Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda

    WhyThe stories were oral for centuries and were recorded in Iceland in the 1200s, the Prose Edda by Snorri Sturluson.

  2. What is Yggdrasil?

    Answer and why

    The world tree that holds the nine worlds.

    WhyIt is the centre and frame of the Norse universe.

  3. Which god gives English the word "Thursday"?

    Answer and why

    Thor

    WhyThursday is "Thor's day". The English, as a Germanic people, once shared these gods.

  4. Why did Tyr put his hand in Fenrir's mouth?

    Answer and why

    As a pledge that the gods were not tricking the wolf.

    WhyFenrir demanded proof of good faith, and only Tyr was brave enough to give it, knowing he would lose his hand.

  5. What does the kenning "whale-road" mean?

    Answer and why

    The sea

    WhyA kenning names something by describing it: the sea is the road along which whales travel.

  6. Give one way in which Norse gods differ from Greek gods.

    Answer and why

    Norse gods are mortal and doomed. Greek gods live for ever.

    WhyRagnarök is foretold as the death of the gods, whereas the Olympians cannot die.

★ Scholar's challenge

The gods bound Fenrir by deceiving him. Were they right to do so? Argue your view in a paragraph with evidence from the story.

Answer and why

Either view can be defended. Right: they protected all the worlds from a known danger. Wrong: they broke faith with a creature who had not yet done harm, and their own god of law paid for it.

WhyA strong answer weighs safety against honesty, uses Tyr's lost hand as evidence that the story itself sees a cost, and notes that fate was not changed in the end.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Is it braver to fight when you might win, or when you know you will lose?
  • Loki causes most of the gods' problems and solves many of them. Do stories need a trickster?
  • The gods try to cheat fate by binding Fenrir, and that act helps bring fate about. Can trying to prevent something cause it?
