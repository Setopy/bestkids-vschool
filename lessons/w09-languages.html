Ω Languages · Week 9

Etymology Mapping

The big questionThe word "chocolate" was born in Mexico, "algebra" in Baghdad, "window" on a Viking ship and "school" in ancient Athens. Every word you speak has travelled. Can you read a word's passport?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Define etymology and read an etymology in a dictionary.
  2. Describe the main historical layers of English vocabulary.
  3. Trace loanwords to their source languages, including Nahuatl and Arabic.
  4. Explain why English often has several words for one idea.

1Every word has a history

Etymology is the study of where words come from and how their form and meaning have changed. The word is Greek: etymon (true sense) and -logia (study).

Words reach a language in two ways. Some are inherited, passed down from the parent language, like "mother" and "three" (Week 1). Others are borrowed from other languages. A borrowed word is called a loanword, though it is never given back.

A dictionary shows etymology in brackets, working backwards. For example: muscle [from French muscle, from Latin musculus, "little mouse"]. The Romans thought a flexing muscle looked like a mouse moving under the skin.

2The layers of English

English is like a cliff face made of layers laid down one after another.

The hundred most common English words are almost all Old English. Yet more than half of the dictionary comes from French and Latin.

Window is Old Norse vindauga, "wind-eye": the hole in the roof that let smoke out and wind in.

Spanish has layers too. Its core is Latin. About eight centuries of Arabic-speaking rule in Spain left some four thousand words, many beginning with al-, the Arabic for "the": almohada (pillow), azúcar (sugar), aceite (oil), and ojalá (God willing). In Mexico, Spanish took in Nahuatl words such as tomate, aguacate and chocolate, and then passed them to the world.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Map the journey of the word "zero".

  1. Sanskrit, in India: shunya, "empty", the name for the place-holder invented by Indian mathematicians.
  2. Arabic: translated as sifr, "empty", when scholars in Baghdad adopted Indian numerals.
  3. Medieval Latin, in Italy: zephirum.
  4. Italian: shortened to zero. Then French and English.

Answer: India, then the Arab world, then Italy, then France and England. The word followed the same road as the number system itself. (The same Arabic word also gave English "cipher".)

Hands-on: The Word Atlas

You need: a printed or drawn world map, coloured thread or pens, pins or stickers, a dictionary with etymologies (printed or online)

  1. Choose twelve words: three foods, three science words, three everyday words, and three from your own home or kitchen.
  2. Look up each etymology. Write it on a small label with the source language.
  3. Place each label on the map where the word was born. Draw its route to where you live.
  4. Add five Spanish words of Arabic origin and five of Nahuatl origin.
  5. Study the finished map. Which regions gave you the most words, and what kinds? What does this tell you about trade, conquest and learning?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is etymology?

    Answer and why

    The study of the origin and history of words.

    WhyIt traces how a word's form and meaning have changed, and which languages it has passed through.

  2. What is a loanword? Give one English example and its source.

    Answer and why

    A word borrowed from another language, such as "chocolate" from Nahuatl.

    WhyLanguages in contact through trade, conquest or learning take words from each other.

  3. Why does English call the animal "cow" and the meat "beef"?

    Answer and why

    "Cow" is Old English, used by farmers. "Beef" comes from the French spoken by the Norman lords.

    WhyAfter 1066 the people who raised the animals spoke English and the people who dined on them spoke French.

  4. From which language did Spanish take almohada and azúcar, and what does al- mean?

    Answer and why

    Arabic. al- means "the".

    WhyCenturies of Arabic-speaking rule in Spain left thousands of words in Spanish.

  5. "Kingly", "royal" and "regal" mean nearly the same. Match each to its layer.

    Answer and why

    kingly: Old English; royal: French; regal: Latin

    WhyEnglish kept its own word and later added the French and Latin ones, each with a slightly different tone.

  6. Which layer of English supplies most of the commonest everyday words?

    Answer and why

    Old English, the Germanic core

    WhyWords like the, and, house, water, eat are inherited and have been in constant use for 1,500 years.

★ Scholar's challenge

Using roots and layers from all nine weeks, explain the origin and literal meaning of each word: (a) Mesopotamia, (b) bilingual, (c) centimetre, (d) monarch, (e) Thursday.

Answer and why

(a) Greek "between rivers"; (b) Latin "two tongues"; (c) Latin "hundredth" plus Greek "measure"; (d) Greek "one ruler"; (e) Old English "Thor's day".

Whymeso + potamos (History, Week 6); bi- + lingua (Week 4); centi- divides, as Latin prefixes do (Week 5); mono- + -arch (Week 4); a Norse god kept in a Germanic language (Literature, Week 4).

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • Is a language with many borrowed words less "pure"? Is purity in a language worth wanting?
