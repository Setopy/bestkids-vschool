✒ Literature · Week 6

Oral Tradition

The big questionBefore writing, a single storyteller could recite a poem of more than fifteen thousand lines from memory. Today most of us cannot remember a shopping list. How did they do it, and what did stories gain from living only in the voice?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Explain what oral tradition is and who its keepers were.
  2. Identify the memory devices used in oral storytelling.
  3. Explain why oral stories exist in many versions.
  4. Learn and perform a short piece from memory.

1Literature before letters

For most of human history nobody could read. Everything a people knew, including its history, laws, family lines, prayers and stories, was carried in memory and passed on by speech. This is oral tradition.

Most societies trained specialists for the task.

KeeperWhereRole
Bard, or rhapsodeAncient GreeceSang epics such as the Iliad and Odyssey, attributed to Homer
Griot, or jeliWest AfricaHistorian, praise-singer and adviser to kings; keeper of the epic of Sundiata
SkaldViking ScandinaviaCourt poet
Elder storytellersPeoples on every continentKeepers of law, land and ancestry

Among the Mande peoples of West Africa a griot is born into the role and studies for years. It is said that when a griot dies, a library burns.

2How to remember ten thousand lines

Oral poets did not memorise word for word as we would a script. They composed as they performed, using tools that make language easy to hold and to rebuild.

DeviceWhat it isExample
Rhythm and metreA steady beatThe six-beat line of Greek epic
RepetitionWhole lines or scenes repeatedA refrain after each episode
EpithetsFixed descriptive phrases"swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered dawn"
FormulasReady-made phrases that fit the beat"When early dawn appeared…"
The rule of threeEvents come in threesThree wishes, three brothers, three trials
Rhyme and alliterationRepeated sounds"Peter Piper picked…"
Call and responseThe audience answers the tellerCommon in African and Caribbean telling

An epithet gave the poet a moment to think of the next line, and gave the listener a familiar handhold. Notice that every device is a kind of pattern. The mind holds patterns far better than it holds loose facts.

3Stories that breathe

A written story is fixed. An oral story is alive: each teller shapes it for the audience in front of them. A tale may grow, shrink, change its hero's name, or swap a wolf for a tiger as it travels. This is why tales like Cinderella exist in hundreds of versions across the world, and why there is often no single "correct" one.

Oral literature is also a performance. Voice, gesture, pauses, music and the listeners' replies are all part of the work, and the page cannot capture them.

4What writing changed

Writing made stories permanent and exact, and let them travel across centuries without a human chain. But the Greek philosopher Socrates warned that writing would weaken memory, because people would trust marks on a page instead of knowledge held in the mind. He never wrote a book. We know his warning only because his student Plato wrote it down.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Find the oral devices in this opening: "Long ago, when the river still spoke, there lived three sisters. The first was strong, the second was swift, and the third was small but clever. Three times the drought came, and three times the sisters went to the river."

  1. Formula: "Long ago, when the river still spoke" is a ready-made opening.
  2. Rule of three: three sisters, three droughts, three journeys.
  3. Repetition with parallel phrasing: "The first was… the second was… the third was…"
  4. Pattern with a twist: the third sister is described differently, a signal that she will be the one who succeeds.

Answer: A formula, the rule of three and repetition. Each one makes the passage easy to remember and to predict.

Hands-on: The Chain of Voices

You need: a short folktale or poem, at least three people, a recording device if you have one

  1. Learn a short folktale well enough to tell it without the page. Do not memorise the words. Memorise the pattern: who, what happens three times, how it ends.
  2. Tell it privately to one person. That person tells it to a third, who tells it back to you. Record the last telling if you can.
  3. List what changed, what was lost and what was added.
  4. Now memorise eight to twelve lines of a poem exactly, using rhythm and repetition. Recite it standing up, from memory.
  5. Which was easier to keep: the exact words or the pattern? Why?

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is oral tradition?

    Answer and why

    The passing on of stories, history and knowledge by speech and memory, without writing.

    WhyBefore writing was common, speech was the only way a society could keep what it knew.

  2. Who are the griots?

    Answer and why

    The hereditary storytellers, historians and praise-singers of West Africa.

    WhyThey preserve family lines and epics such as that of Sundiata, founder of the Mali Empire.

  3. What is an epithet? Give an example.

    Answer and why

    A fixed descriptive phrase attached to a name, such as "swift-footed Achilles".

    WhyEpithets fit the rhythm of the line and help both poet and listener remember.

  4. Why do many folktales use the rule of three?

    Answer and why

    Three is the smallest number that makes a pattern and then lets it be broken.

    WhyTwo events set up an expectation and the third confirms or overturns it, which is satisfying and easy to remember.

  5. Why are there many versions of the same oral tale?

    Answer and why

    Each teller adapts it, and small changes build up as it passes from person to person.

    WhyWith no fixed written text, the story is remade at every telling.

  6. Name one thing a story gains and one thing it loses when it is written down.

    Answer and why

    It gains permanence and exactness. It loses the performance and the freedom to change.

    WhyWriting fixes the words, but voice, gesture and the teller's response to the audience do not reach the page.

★ Scholar's challenge

Socrates argued that writing would make people forgetful. Today some people say the same about the internet and artificial intelligence. Write a paragraph that takes a side, using what you have learned about memory devices and oral tradition.

Answer and why

Either side can be argued. For: skills we do not practise fade, as shown by the vast memories of oral poets. Against: writing freed the mind for new kinds of thinking and preserved knowledge that memory would have lost.

WhyA strong answer makes a clear claim, uses at least one piece of evidence from the lesson, and admits what the other side gets right.

Words to own

Oral tradition
Knowledge and stories passed on by word of mouth.
Bard
A poet who performs stories or songs.
Griot
A West African oral historian and storyteller.
Epithet
A descriptive phrase regularly attached to a name.
Formula
A set phrase reused by oral poets.
Version
One particular form of a story that exists in several forms.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • If a story changes every time it is told, is it still the same story?
  • Which do you trust more: a written record or a person's memory? When might memory be the better witness?
  • What stories does your own family pass on by voice? Who is the keeper of them?
