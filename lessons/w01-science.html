⚛ Science · Week 1

Metrology & SI Units

The big questionIn 1999 a spacecraft worth 125 million dollars was lost at Mars because two teams used different units. How can a unit destroy a spacecraft?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Explain why science needs one shared system of measurement.
  2. Name the seven SI base units and what each one measures.
  3. Use the prefixes kilo, centi, milli and micro to convert measurements.
  4. Tell the difference between accuracy and precision, and between mass and weight.

1Why measurement needs a standard

Metrology is the science of measurement. To measure is to compare something with an agreed standard, called a unit.

For most of history, units were local. A "foot" was the length of a king's foot, and a new king meant a new foot. Trade and science were full of arguments. During the 1790s French scientists designed the metric system, built on tens. Its modern form is the International System of Units, or SI, used by scientists in every country.

The Mars Climate Orbiter shows what is at stake. One team sent thruster data in pound-force seconds. The navigation software expected newton seconds. Nobody converted. The spacecraft flew too low into the Martian atmosphere and was lost.

Every other unit is built from these. Speed is metres per second. Volume is cubic metres. Since 2019 every base unit is defined by a constant of nature. A metre, for example, is the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second, so any laboratory on Earth can reproduce it.

To convert to a smaller unit you need more of them, so multiply. To convert to a larger unit you need fewer, so divide.

4Accuracy, precision, mass and weight

Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other. A scale that always reads 2 kg too heavy is precise but not accurate.

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms. Weight is the pull of gravity on that mass, measured in newtons. On the Moon your mass is unchanged, but your weight is about one sixth of your weight on Earth.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

Convert 3.5 km to metres.

  1. Kilo means × 1,000.
  2. Metres are smaller than kilometres, so we expect more of them: multiply.
  3. 3.5 × 1,000 = 3,500.

Answer: 3.5 km = 3,500 m

Example 2

Convert 250 mL to litres.

  1. Milli means ÷ 1,000.
  2. Litres are larger, so we expect fewer: divide.
  3. 250 ÷ 1,000 = 0.25.

Answer: 250 mL = 0.25 L

Hands-on: The Body-Unit Problem

You need: string, scissors, a metre rule or tape measure, notebook

  1. Cut a string as long as your own foot. Cut another as long as a parent's foot.
  2. Measure the length of a room in "child feet" and then in "parent feet". Record both.
  3. Explain in one sentence why two honest people got different numbers.
  4. Measure the room again with the metre rule, three times. Are your three results precise? How could you tell whether they are accurate?
  5. Estimate, then measure: the width of a fingernail (cm), the height of a door (m), the mass of an apple (g).

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. Convert 4,200 g to kilograms.

    Answer and why

    4.2 kg

    WhyA kilogram is 1,000 g. Kilograms are larger, so there are fewer of them: 4,200 ÷ 1,000 = 4.2.

  2. Convert 0.07 m to millimetres.

    Answer and why

    70 mm

    WhyThere are 1,000 mm in a metre. Millimetres are smaller, so multiply: 0.07 × 1,000 = 70.

  3. How many seconds are in 2 hours 15 minutes?

    Answer and why

    8,100 s

    Why2 h 15 min is 135 minutes, and 135 × 60 = 8,100.

  4. Which SI base unit would you use for the temperature of a star?

    Answer and why

    The kelvin

    WhyThe kelvin is the SI base unit of temperature. Its zero is absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature, so it never needs negative numbers.

  5. A dart player hits the same spot five times, but far from the bullseye. Precise, accurate, both or neither?

    Answer and why

    Precise but not accurate

    WhyThe throws agree with each other (precise) but are far from the true target (not accurate).

  6. An astronaut has a mass of 70 kg on Earth. What is her mass on the Moon?

    Answer and why

    70 kg

    WhyMass is the amount of matter, and travelling does not remove matter. Only her weight changes, because the Moon's gravity is weaker.

  7. Put in order from smallest to largest: 5 cm, 0.5 m, 55 mm, 0.0005 km.

    Answer and why

    5 cm, 55 mm, 0.5 m and 0.0005 km (the last two are equal)

    WhyIn metres they are 0.05, 0.055, 0.5 and 0.5. Converting everything to one unit is the only safe way to compare.

★ Scholar's challenge

A human hair is about 80 µm wide. How many hairs laid side by side would span 1 cm?

Answer and why

125 hairs

Why1 cm = 10 mm = 10,000 µm. Then 10,000 ÷ 80 = 125.

Words to own

Metrology
The science of measurement.
SI
The International System of Units, the world standard for science.
Base unit
One of the seven units from which all other SI units are built.
Prefix
A word-part placed before a unit to multiply or divide it by a power of ten.
Precision
How closely repeated measurements agree with each other.
Accuracy
How close a measurement is to the true value.
