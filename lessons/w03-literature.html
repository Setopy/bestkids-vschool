Greek & Roman Myth

The big questionWe call a weakness an "Achilles' heel", a long trip an "odyssey" and a huge ship "titanic". Why are stories told 2,800 years ago still hiding inside the words we use today?

By the end of this lesson you will be able to

  1. Explain what a myth is and what it did for the people who told it.
  2. Name the major Olympian gods by their Greek and Roman names.
  3. Retell three myths and state the idea each one teaches.
  4. Recognise and explain allusions to Greek myth.

1What myths are for

A myth is a traditional story that a people told to explain the world and their place in it. The ancient Greeks had no microscopes or weather maps. Their myths answered the questions every people asks: Why do the seasons change? Where did fire come from? Why do proud people fall?

Today we read these stories as literature. Nobody worships Zeus, but writers, artists and scientists have borrowed from the myths for more than two thousand years. A reader who knows them holds a key to a great deal of later writing.

Myths do three jobs. They explain nature, they teach what a society values, and they warn about human faults.

The Greek gods are powerful but not good. They are jealous, proud and quarrelsome, like very strong human beings. This makes the myths studies of human character.

3Three myths to know

Demeter and Persephone. Hades carries Persephone, daughter of the harvest goddess Demeter, to the underworld. Demeter grieves and nothing grows. Persephone is allowed to return, but because she ate pomegranate seeds below she must go back for part of each year. Job: explains the seasons.

Prometheus. The Titan Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humankind. Zeus chains him to a rock as punishment. Job: explains how humans gained skill and craft, and asks what the price of progress is.

Daedalus and Icarus. The inventor Daedalus builds wings of feathers and wax to escape from Crete. He warns his son Icarus not to fly too high. Icarus soars toward the sun, the wax melts, and he falls into the sea. Job: warns against hubris.

4Hubris and nemesis

Hubris is pride so great that a person forgets their limits. In Greek stories hubris is always followed by nemesis, the downfall it deserves. Arachne boasts that she weaves better than Athena and is turned into a spider. King Midas wishes for everything he touches to become gold and nearly starves.

5Allusions

An allusion is a brief reference to a well-known story. The writer expects you to recognise it.

Worked examples

Cover the steps. Predict each line before you read it.

Example 1

What does the writer mean? "The new software was a Pandora's box: once we installed it, error after error poured out."

  1. Identify the allusion: Pandora's box.
  2. Recall the myth: Pandora opened a container and troubles flew out into the world and could not be put back.
  3. Apply it: installing the software is the opening, and the errors are the troubles.

Answer: Installing it caused many problems that could not easily be undone.

Hands-on: Myth Retold

You need: paper, pencils, any collection of Greek myths

  1. Read one myth not described above (try Perseus, Theseus, Orpheus or Atalanta).
  2. Fill in a four-box page: characters, the problem, the turning point, the ending.
  3. Decide which job the myth does: explain, teach or warn. Write one sentence of evidence.
  4. Retell it aloud in under three minutes without notes.
  5. Find one modern word, brand or space mission named after a figure from Greek or Roman myth.

Practice

Write your full working in your notebook before opening an answer.

  1. What is a myth?

    Answer and why

    A traditional story a people told to explain the world, teach values or warn about faults.

    WhyMyths were a culture's shared answers to large questions before science and written history.

  2. Give the Roman names of Zeus, Poseidon and Athena.

    Answer and why

    Jupiter, Neptune and Minerva

    WhyThe Romans took over the Greek gods under Latin names, and those names passed into European languages and astronomy.

  3. Which natural event does the story of Persephone explain?

    Answer and why

    The cycle of the seasons

    WhyCrops die when Demeter mourns her daughter's absence and grow again when Persephone returns.

  4. What is hubris? Give one example from a myth.

    Answer and why

    Excessive pride that ignores limits, for example Icarus flying too near the sun.

    WhyHe ignored his father's warning, trusting his own power, and his fall is the nemesis that follows.

  5. Explain the allusion in "Mathematics was his Achilles' heel."

    Answer and why

    Mathematics was his one serious weakness.

    WhyAchilles was invulnerable everywhere except the heel, so the phrase means a single weak spot in someone otherwise strong.

  6. Why are the Greek gods described as "human-like"?

    Answer and why

    They show human emotions and faults such as jealousy, anger and pride.

    WhyTheir stories explore human character on a grand scale, which is why they still interest readers.

★ Scholar's challenge

Prometheus is punished for giving fire to humans. Was he a hero or a criminal? Write a paragraph arguing one side, using details from the myth.

Answer and why

Either side can be defended. Hero: he suffered to help the weak. Criminal: he broke the law of his ruler and upset the order of the world.

WhyA strong answer states a claim, gives at least two pieces of evidence from the story, and answers the best argument of the other side.

Socratic table talk

Discuss aloud. There is no answer key: defend your thinking with reasons.

  • The Greeks made their gods flawed. What does that tell you about how they saw power?
  • Why might an ancient story explain the seasons with a family quarrel instead of with the tilt of the Earth?
  • Is Icarus foolish or brave? Would there be any discoveries without people who fly too high?
