@lesson 7 math Sequences
@hook A teacher once told a class of small boys to add every number from 1 to 100, hoping for a quiet hour. Seven-year-old Carl Gauss wrote down 5,050 within moments. He did not add faster than the others. What did he see?
@goals
- Recognise arithmetic and geometric sequences and state their rules.
- Find a position-to-term rule (the nth term) for an arithmetic sequence.
- Describe the square, triangular and Fibonacci numbers.
- Add a long run of consecutive numbers using Gauss's pairing method.
@section Patterns with rules
A **sequence** is an ordered list of numbers that follows a rule. Each number is a **term**.
- In an **arithmetic sequence** you **add** the same number each time. That number is the **common difference**. Example: 5, 8, 11, 14 … adds 3.
- In a **geometric sequence** you **multiply** by the same number each time. That number is the **common ratio**. Example: 3, 6, 12, 24 … multiplies by 2.
To test a sequence, subtract neighbours. If the differences are equal, it is arithmetic. If not, divide neighbours. If the quotients are equal, it is geometric.
@section Two kinds of rule
A **term-to-term rule** tells you how to get the next term from the one before: "add 3". It is easy to find, but to reach the 100th term you would need 99 steps.
A **position-to-term rule** gives any term directly from its position, n. It is called the **nth term**.
For 5, 8, 11, 14 … the sequence rises by 3 each time, like the 3 times table: 3, 6, 9, 12. Each term is 2 more than the matching multiple of 3. So the nth term is **3n + 2**.
| Position n | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | n |
| 3 × n | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 3n |
| Term | 5 | 8 | 11 | 14 | 3n + 2 |
**Method:** the common difference becomes the multiplier of n. Then adjust by whatever makes the first term correct.
@section Famous sequences
| Name | Terms | Rule |
| Square numbers | 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 … | n × n |
| Triangular numbers | 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 … | Add 2, then 3, then 4 …; the nth is n × (n + 1) ÷ 2 |
| Fibonacci numbers | 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 … | Each term is the sum of the two before it |
| Powers of 2 | 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … | Double each time |
The Fibonacci numbers appear in the spirals of sunflowers, pine cones and pineapples. The differences between neighbouring square numbers are the odd numbers: 3, 5, 7, 9 …
@section Gauss's trick
To add 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 100, Gauss paired the first with the last, the second with the second-last, and so on.
1 + 100 = 101, 2 + 99 = 101, 3 + 98 = 101 …
There are 50 pairs, each worth 101, and 50 × 101 = 5,050.
In general, the sum of 1 to n is **n × (n + 1) ÷ 2**. This is the formula for the triangular numbers, because the nth triangular number is exactly 1 + 2 + … + n.
@example Find the nth term of 4, 9, 14, 19 … and use it to find the 50th term.
- The common difference is 5, so the rule starts with 5n.
- The 5 times table gives 5, 10, 15, 20. Each term of our sequence is 1 less.
- The nth term is 5n − 1.
- 50th term: 5 × 50 − 1 = 249.
= nth term 5n − 1; the 50th term is 249
@example Is 100 a term of the sequence 3, 7, 11, 15 …?
- The common difference is 4, and the first term is 3, so the nth term is 4n − 1.
- If 100 is a term, then 4n − 1 = 100, so 4n = 101.
- 101 is not a multiple of 4.
= No. (99 and 103 are terms, but 100 is not.)

- Write the nth term. Use it to predict the sticks for 10 squares, then build and check.
- Repeat with a row of joined triangles.
- On squared paper, draw the first five triangular numbers as dot triangles. Put two copies of one together to make a rectangle. How does that show n × (n + 1) ÷ 2?

@lesson 7 science Separation Techniques
@hook Sea water is a mixture of water and salt that no sieve in the world can separate. Yet a sailor stranded on a raft could make drinking water from it with a plastic sheet and the Sun. How?
@goals
- Distinguish a pure substance from a mixture, and a solution from other mixtures.
- Choose a separation method according to the properties of the substances.
- Explain filtration, evaporation, distillation and chromatography with particles.
- Plan a multi-step separation.
@section Mixtures and solutions
A **pure substance** contains only one kind of substance: one element or one compound. A **mixture** contains two or more substances that are **not chemically bonded**. Each keeps its own properties, so a mixture can be separated by **physical** means.
A **solution** is a mixture in which one substance has dissolved in another so evenly that you cannot see the parts.
| Term | Meaning | In salt water |
| Solute | The substance that dissolves | Salt |
| Solvent | The liquid it dissolves in | Water |
| Solution | The mixture formed | Salt water |
| Soluble | Able to dissolve | Salt is soluble in water |
| Insoluble | Not able to dissolve | Sand is insoluble in water |
@section Match the method to the property
Every separation uses a **difference in a physical property**. First ask: what is different about these substances?
| Method | Separates | Property used |
| Sieving | Solids of different sizes | Particle size |
| Magnetic attraction | Iron or steel from other materials | Magnetism |
| Decanting | A liquid from a solid that has settled | Density |
| Filtration | An insoluble solid from a liquid | Particle size |
| Evaporation | A dissolved solid from its solution | Boiling point |
| Distillation | A solvent from a solution, or two liquids | Boiling point |
| Chromatography | Several dissolved substances, such as dyes | How strongly each clings to paper |
@section How the main methods work
**Filtration.** Filter paper has holes too small for solid grains but large enough for water particles and dissolved particles. The solid left on the paper is the **residue**. The liquid that passes through is the **filtrate**.
**Evaporation.** Heat the solution. The solvent particles escape as gas, and the solute is left behind as crystals. You keep the solid and lose the liquid.
**Distillation.** Heat the solution so the solvent boils, lead the vapour through a cooled tube where it condenses, and collect the pure liquid, the **distillate**. You keep the liquid. This is how fresh water is made from sea water.
**Chromatography.** Put a spot of ink near the bottom of a strip of absorbent paper and stand the end in water. As the water climbs it carries the dyes. Dyes that dissolve well travel far. Dyes that cling to the paper lag behind. The ink separates into bands of colour.
@section Planning a separation
Real mixtures need several steps in the right order. Deal with one substance at a time, and ask at each step what property makes it different from the rest.
@example Plan the separation of a mixture of sand, salt and iron filings so that all three are recovered dry.
- Iron is magnetic and the others are not. Pass a magnet over the mixture to remove the iron filings.
- Salt is soluble and sand is not. Add warm water and stir until the salt dissolves.
- Filter. The sand is the residue. The salt solution is the filtrate.
- Dry the sand. Evaporate the water from the filtrate to leave salt crystals.
= Magnet, then dissolve, then filter, then evaporate. Each step uses a different property.
@activity Ink Detective and the Solar Still | white coffee filters or kitchen paper, water-based felt-tip pens (include black), a glass, water, a large bowl, a small cup, cling film, a pebble, salt

- Hang the strip in a glass with the end in 1 cm of water, the dot above the water. Watch for 10 minutes. How many colours are in "black"? Test three other pens.

@lesson 7 history Ancient Egypt
@hook Egypt lasted for almost three thousand years. Cleopatra lived closer in time to us than to the builders of the Great Pyramid. What allowed one civilisation to endure so long?
@goals
- Explain how the Nile shaped Egyptian life and belief.
- Describe the role of the pharaoh and the structure of Egyptian society.
- Explain how and why the pyramids were built.
- Describe hieroglyphs and how they were deciphered.
@section The gift of the Nile
Egypt is a desert with one river running through it. The **Nile** flows north for about 6,650 km from the heart of Africa to the Mediterranean, where it fans out in a delta. The Greek historian Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the Nile".
Each summer, rains far to the south made the river rise and flood, on almost the same dates every year. When the water went down it left a strip of rich black silt. Egyptians called this farmland **Kemet**, the Black Land, and the desert beyond **Deshret**, the Red Land. Their year had three seasons.
| Season | Months (approx.) | What happened |
| Akhet (flood) | July to October | Fields under water; farmers worked on royal building projects |
| Peret (growing) | November to February | Ploughing and sowing in the fresh mud |
| Shemu (harvest) | March to June | Reaping and storing grain |
Compare this with Mesopotamia, where floods were violent and unpredictable. Egypt's river was regular, and the deserts and sea protected it from invasion. Egyptians came to see the world as orderly and lasting. Their word for this right order, truth and balance was **ma'at**.
Because the Nile flows north, the south is called **Upper Egypt** (upstream) and the north **Lower Egypt** (downstream).
@section The pharaoh
About 3100 BC a king traditionally called **Narmer**, or Menes, united Upper and Lower Egypt. The ruler, later called **pharaoh**, wore a double crown and was believed to be a living god, responsible for keeping ma'at. He owned the land, commanded the army and directed the storing of grain against bad years.
| Rank | Who |
| 1 | Pharaoh |
| 2 | Vizier (chief minister), priests, nobles |
| 3 | Scribes and officials |
| 4 | Craftspeople and merchants |
| 5 | Farmers, the great majority |
| 6 | Servants and enslaved people |
Historians divide Egypt's history into the **Old Kingdom** (the age of pyramids), the **Middle Kingdom** and the **New Kingdom** (the age of empire, with rulers such as Hatshepsut, Tutankhamun and Ramses II).
@section Houses of eternity
Egyptians believed life continued after death if the body was preserved and provided for. Bodies were dried and wrapped as **mummies**, and tombs were filled with food, furniture and treasure.
The pyramids are royal tombs of the Old Kingdom. The **Great Pyramid of Giza** was built for the pharaoh Khufu about 2560 BC. It contained about 2.3 million stone blocks, stood about 146 m tall, and was the tallest structure on Earth for nearly four thousand years. Its sides are lined up almost perfectly with north, south, east and west.
It was not built by slaves. Archaeologists have found the workers' town, with bakeries, breweries and doctors. The builders were paid, organised crews, joined in the flood season by farmers who owed labour to the king.
@section Sacred writing
Egyptians wrote in **hieroglyphs**, picture signs that could stand for a whole word or for a sound. Scribes wrote with reed pens on **papyrus**, sheets made from a river reed. Our word "paper" comes from it.
After Egypt fell under Roman and later rule, the knowledge of hieroglyphs was lost for about 1,400 years. In 1799 French soldiers found the **Rosetta Stone**, which carries one decree in three scripts: hieroglyphic, a later Egyptian script, and Greek. Scholars could read the Greek. In 1822 **Jean-François Champollion** used it to crack the code, and Egypt could speak again.
@section Lasting knowledge
- A **365-day calendar**, based on the flood and the star Sirius.
- Practical **geometry**, for re-measuring fields after each flood and for building.
- **Medicine**: written manuals on wounds, fractures and remedies.
- Monumental **architecture** in stone.
@example Explain how the Nile's flood helped make the pyramids possible.
- The flood renewed the soil every year, giving large and reliable harvests.
- The surplus grain was collected and stored by the pharaoh's officials.
- During the flood months farmers could not work their fields.
- The pharaoh used the stored grain to feed and pay those farmers to build.
= The Nile supplied both the food surplus and the free labour season that great building projects required.

@lesson 7 literature Protagonists
@hook A good author never writes "Mara was brave." The author shows Mara's hands shaking as she opens the door anyway. Why is the second version so much stronger, and how do writers make people out of ink?
@goals
- Identify the protagonist and antagonist of a story.
- Distinguish direct from indirect characterisation.
- Analyse a character through speech, thoughts, effect on others, actions and looks.
- Describe a character's motivation, flaw and arc.
@section Who the story is about
The **protagonist** is the central character, the one whose problem drives the story and whose choices we follow. The word is Greek: *protos* (first) and *agonistes* (contestant, actor). The **antagonist** is the force that stands against the protagonist. It may be a person, a society, nature, or something inside the protagonist.
The protagonist is not always good, and the antagonist is not always evil. "Protagonist" means central, not virtuous.
@section Telling and showing
An author builds a character in two ways.
**Direct characterisation** tells you the trait: "Tomás was generous."
**Indirect characterisation** shows evidence and trusts you to infer the trait: "Tomás broke his bread in two and held out the larger piece."
Good readers gather the evidence with five questions, remembered as **STEAL**.
| Letter | Ask | Example |
| **S**peech | What does the character say, and how? | "I suppose I could help," she muttered. |
| **T**houghts | What does the character think and feel in private? | He counted the exits. |
| **E**ffect on others | How do other characters react? | The room fell silent when she entered. |
| **A**ctions | What does the character do? | He returned the wallet without opening it. |
| **L**ooks | What do appearance and manner suggest? | Her boots were patched but polished. |
Actions weigh the most. When a character's words and actions disagree, believe the actions.
@section Depth and change
| Term | Meaning |
| Flat character | Built on one trait; simple |
| Round character | Has several traits, some in conflict; feels real |
| Static character | Stays the same from beginning to end |
| Dynamic character | Changes in an important way |
A **motivation** is what a character wants and why. A **flaw** is a weakness that causes trouble. The path of change from the beginning to the end is the **character arc**. In most strong stories the protagonist is round and dynamic: they want something, their flaw stands in the way, and the events of the plot force them to change.
To describe an arc, complete this sentence: "At first ___ is ___, but after ___, they become ___."
@example Analyse this passage with STEAL. "Old Bao arrived at the school gate an hour before the bell, as he had every day for thirty years. He straightened the crooked sign, though nobody had asked him to. When the new headmaster hurried past without a word, Bao only bowed. 'Fine morning,' he said to the empty yard. The smallest children ran to him first."
- Speech: "Fine morning", said to an empty yard. He is cheerful and expects no reply.
- Thoughts: none are given. We must infer everything.
- Effect on others: the smallest children run to him first. He is trusted and loved by those who judge by kindness.
- Actions: he comes an hour early for thirty years, straightens the sign unasked, and bows to rudeness. He is dutiful, takes pride in his work, and is patient.
- Looks: "Old" is the only detail. The author relies on action.
= Bao is loyal, humble and kind. The author never states any of these traits. Every one is shown.
@activity Character Dossier | a novel you are reading, a notebook, pencils
- Name the protagonist and the antagonist. Is the antagonist a person, a group, nature or an inner struggle?
- Rule a page into five boxes for STEAL. Find one quotation for each, with its page number.
- From your evidence write three trait words. Underline the evidence for each.
- Write the protagonist's motivation in one sentence and their flaw in another.
- Draw the arc as a line from the first chapter to where you are now. Mark the events that changed the character.
@practice

= No. It is whatever opposes the protagonist, and may be nature, society or an inner conflict.

= Indirect
! The author shows an action and leaves us to infer that Lucía is generous, or self-sacrificing.

= Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks

= A static character does not change. A dynamic character changes in an important way.
! The change is usually in belief, attitude or understanding, caused by the events of the story.

@vocab

@section Every noun has a gender
In French, as in Spanish, every noun is either **masculine** or **feminine**. You must learn each noun together with its article.
| | Masculine | Feminine | Plural |
| the | le | la | les |

Before a vowel or silent h, *le* and *la* both shorten to **l'**: *l'oncle*, *l'école*. Compare the Spanish *el, la, los, las*.

Say them: *deux* "duh", *trois* "trwa", *cinq* "sank", *six* "seess", *sept* "set", *huit* "weet", *dix* "deess", *vingt* "van". From 17 to 19 French simply says "ten-seven, ten-eight, ten-nine".

For 21, 31, 41, 51 and 61 say *et un*: *vingt et un*. Otherwise join with a hyphen: *vingt-deux*, *trente-cinq*. After 60, keep counting on from sixty: 71 is *soixante et onze*, 75 is *soixante-quinze*. After 80, count on from four-twenties: 91 is *quatre-vingt-onze*, 99 is *quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*.
This is a fossil of **base twenty**. The Celtic peoples who lived in France before the Romans counted in twenties, perhaps on fingers and toes. English has a fossil too: "four score and seven" means 87. In Belgium and Switzerland French speakers say *septante* for 70 and *nonante* for 90.
@example Say 78 and 94 in French.
- 78 is 60 + 18: *soixante* + *dix-huit*.
- 94 is 80 + 14: *quatre-vingt* + *quatorze*.
= soixante-dix-huit; quatre-vingt-quatorze
@example Translate: "J'ai deux frères. Ma sœur a sept ans. Mes grands-parents ont une maison."
- *J'ai* is "I have". *deux frères* is "two brothers".
- *Ma sœur a sept ans*: "my sister has seven years", that is, she is seven.
- *ont* is "they have". *une maison* is "a house".
= "I have two brothers. My sister is seven years old. My grandparents have a house."
