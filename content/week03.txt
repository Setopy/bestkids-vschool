@lesson 3 math Order of Operations
@hook Type 8 + 4 × 3 into a cheap calculator and you may get 36. A scientific calculator gives 20. Both machines can add and multiply perfectly. So which one is wrong, and who says so?
@goals
- State the agreed order of operations and explain why an agreement is needed.
- Evaluate expressions with brackets, exponents and all four operations.
- Work multiplication and division, and addition and subtraction, from left to right.
- Insert brackets to make a statement true.
@section Why we need an agreement
An **expression** such as 8 + 4 × 3 is a sentence in the language of mathematics. If readers could do the steps in any order, the same sentence would have several meanings. Mathematicians everywhere therefore follow one **convention**, an agreed rule.
The rule is not random. Multiplication is repeated addition, so 4 × 3 is a packed bundle (4 + 4 + 4) that must be opened before joining it to anything else. Exponents are repeated multiplication, so they are packed even tighter.
@section The order
| Step | Operation | Note |
| 1 | **Brackets** | Innermost first |
| 2 | **Exponents** | Powers such as 5² |
| 3 | **Multiplication and division** | Equal rank: work left to right |
| 4 | **Addition and subtraction** | Equal rank: work left to right |
You may know a memory word such as BODMAS, BIDMAS or PEMDAS. Be careful: the word makes it look as if division comes before multiplication, or addition before subtraction. They do not. **Partners of equal rank are done in the order they appear, from left to right.**
So 36 ÷ 6 × 3 is (36 ÷ 6) × 3 = 18, not 36 ÷ 18 = 2. And 20 − 5 + 3 is 15 + 3 = 18, not 20 − 8 = 12.
@section Writing a clean solution
Rewrite the whole expression on a new line after every step, changing only one thing at a time. Keep the equals signs lined up. This habit makes mistakes easy to find and is how professional mathematicians write.
A fraction bar acts like brackets: everything above it is one group and everything below is another.
@example Evaluate 5 + 2 × (9 − 4)².
- Brackets: 9 − 4 = 5, giving 5 + 2 × 5².
- Exponent: 5² = 25, giving 5 + 2 × 25.
- Multiplication: 2 × 25 = 50, giving 5 + 50.
- Addition: 55.
= 55
@example Evaluate 48 ÷ (2 + 6) × 3 − 4.
- Brackets: 2 + 6 = 8, giving 48 ÷ 8 × 3 − 4.
- Division and multiplication, left to right: 48 ÷ 8 = 6, then 6 × 3 = 18.
- Subtraction: 18 − 4 = 14.
= 14
@example Insert one pair of brackets to make 4 + 6 × 2 − 1 = 19 true.
- Without brackets: 4 + 12 − 1 = 15. Too small.
- We need the addition to happen first: (4 + 6) × 2 − 1.
- Check: 10 × 2 − 1 = 19. ✓
= (4 + 6) × 2 − 1 = 19
@activity Four Fours | paper, pencil, a timer
- Using exactly four 4s and any operations and brackets, make each whole number from 0 to 10.
- Start with these: 44 ÷ 44 = 1 and (4 + 4 + 4) ÷ 4 = 3.
- Write every expression with correct brackets, then hand it to a partner to evaluate without help.
- If your partner gets a different answer, find out whose order of operations slipped.
- Extension: how far beyond 10 can you go?

= 26
! Both multiplications come before the subtraction: 56 − 30 = 26.

= 20
! Division first: 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Then left to right: 20 − 3 = 17, and 17 + 3 = 20.

= 10
! Exponents first: 8 + 16 ÷ 8. Then division: 8 + 2. Then addition: 10.
? Evaluate 100 − 4 × (3 + 2)².
= 0
! Brackets give 5, the exponent gives 25, the multiplication gives 100, and 100 − 100 = 0.
? Evaluate 36 ÷ 6 × 3.
= 18
! Division and multiplication have equal rank, so work left to right: 6 × 3 = 18.
? Evaluate [18 − (4 + 2) × 2] ÷ 3.
= 2
! Innermost brackets: 6. Inside the square brackets multiply first: 18 − 12 = 6. Then 6 ÷ 3 = 2.
? Insert one pair of brackets to make 3 + 5 × 4 − 2 = 13 true.
= 3 + 5 × (4 − 2) = 13
! The brackets force the subtraction first: 5 × 2 = 10, and 3 + 10 = 13.
@challenge
? Using the digits 2, 3, 4 and 5 once each, in that order, with any operations and brackets, make 14. Then make 25. Then make 45.
= 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 14; (2 + 3) × 4 + 5 = 25; (2 + 3) × (4 + 5) = 45
! For 25, the brackets make the addition happen first: 5 × 4 + 5 = 25. For 45, two pairs of brackets give 5 × 9. The same four digits give very different values depending only on the order of the operations.
@vocab
Expression: Numbers and operations written together, with no equals sign.
Evaluate: To work out the value of an expression.
Convention: A rule everyone agrees to follow so that meaning is shared.
Exponent: A raised number that tells how many times to multiply the base by itself.
Brackets: Symbols that group part of an expression to be done first.

@lesson 3 science Atomic Structure & the Bohr Model
@hook If an atom were the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be a small marble on the centre spot, and almost everything else would be empty. If atoms are nearly all empty space, why can you not walk through a wall?
@goals
- Name the three subatomic particles with their charges and locations.
- Use atomic number and mass number to count protons, neutrons and electrons.
- Draw Bohr diagrams for the first twenty elements.
- Describe how the model of the atom changed as new evidence appeared.
@section Inside the atom
An **atom** is the smallest piece of an element that is still that element. It has a tiny, dense centre called the **nucleus**, surrounded by electrons.
| Particle | Charge | Where | Relative mass |
| Proton | +1 | In the nucleus | 1 |
| Neutron | 0 | In the nucleus | 1 |
| Electron | −1 | Around the nucleus | about 1/1,836 |
A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel. Nearly all the mass is in the nucleus.
You cannot walk through a wall because the electrons of your atoms repel the electrons of the wall's atoms. Like charges push apart.
@section Two numbers that describe an atom
- The **atomic number** is the number of protons. It decides which element the atom is. Six protons is always carbon. Seven is always nitrogen.
- The **mass number** is protons plus neutrons.
- Neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons are called **isotopes**. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8. Both are carbon.
@section The Bohr model
In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed that electrons travel in fixed **shells**, or energy levels, around the nucleus. For the first twenty elements the shells fill in order:
| Shell | Holds up to |
| 1st | 2 electrons |
| 2nd | 8 electrons |
| 3rd | 8 electrons |
| 4th | the rest (for elements 19 and 20) |
We write the result as an **electron arrangement**. Sodium, with 11 electrons, is 2, 8, 1. The electrons in the outermost shell are **valence electrons**. They decide how an atom behaves chemically.
@section A model that kept improving
| Year | Scientist | Idea | Evidence |
| about 400 BC | Democritus | Matter is made of uncuttable "atomos" | Reasoning only |
| 1803 | John Dalton | Each element has its own kind of solid atom | Mass ratios in reactions |
| 1897 | J. J. Thomson | Atoms contain tiny negative electrons | Cathode-ray experiments |
| 1911 | Ernest Rutherford | A tiny, dense, positive nucleus | Gold-foil experiment |
| 1913 | Niels Bohr | Electrons occupy fixed shells | Colours of light given off by hydrogen |
Rutherford fired positive particles at thin gold foil. Most went straight through, so the atom is mostly empty. A few bounced back, so something small, heavy and positive sits at the centre. Scientists now describe electrons as a cloud, but the Bohr model is still the best first picture.
@example An atom of chlorine has atomic number 17 and mass number 35. Find its protons, neutrons and electrons, and its electron arrangement.
- Protons = atomic number = 17.
- Neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18.
- A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons: 17.
- Fill the shells: 2, then 8, leaving 7.
= 17 protons, 18 neutrons, 17 electrons, arranged 2, 8, 7
@activity Build-an-Atom Plate | paper plates, three colours of small sweets, beads or beans, a pair of compasses, a pen
- Draw a small circle in the centre of a plate for the nucleus and three rings around it for shells.
- Choose one colour for protons, one for neutrons and one for electrons.
- Build carbon-12: 6 protons and 6 neutrons in the nucleus, electrons 2, 4.
- Build oxygen-16, sodium-23 and argon-40. Write the electron arrangement under each.
- Which of your atoms has a completely full outer shell? Remember it for next week.
@practice
? Which particle decides what element an atom is?
= The proton
! The number of protons is the atomic number, and each element has its own atomic number.
? An atom has 8 protons, 8 neutrons and 8 electrons. What are its atomic number and mass number?
= Atomic number 8, mass number 16 (it is oxygen)
! Atomic number counts protons. Mass number adds protons and neutrons: 8 + 8.
? Write the electron arrangement of calcium, atomic number 20.
= 2, 8, 8, 2
! Fill shells in order: 2, then 8, then 8 makes 18, leaving 2 for the fourth shell.
? Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes. What is the same and what is different?
= Same: 6 protons (and 6 electrons). Different: 6 neutrons against 8.
! Isotopes are atoms of one element, so the proton number is fixed. Only the neutron count, and therefore the mass, changes.
? In Rutherford's experiment most particles went straight through the foil. What did that show?
= Atoms are mostly empty space.
! If atoms were solid all the way through, the particles would have been blocked or deflected much more often.
? How many valence electrons does sodium (2, 8, 1) have?
= One
! Valence electrons are those in the outermost occupied shell, and sodium's third shell holds a single electron.
@challenge
? Potassium has atomic number 19 and its most common atom has mass number 39. Give its neutrons and electron arrangement. Then compare its outer shell with sodium's and predict whether the two elements behave alike.
= 20 neutrons; arrangement 2, 8, 8, 1. Yes, they behave alike.
! 39 − 19 = 20. Both atoms have a single valence electron, and valence electrons control chemical behaviour. (Both are soft metals that react strongly with water.)
@vocab
Nucleus: The tiny, dense centre of an atom, holding protons and neutrons.
Atomic number: The number of protons in an atom.
Mass number: The total number of protons and neutrons.
Isotope: Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.
Valence electron: An electron in the outermost shell.
Model: A simplified picture used to explain and predict.

- Democritus guessed atoms existed without any experiment. Was that science?
- What kind of evidence would make scientists change the model of the atom again?

@lesson 3 history Biomes
@hook A polar bear and a camel are both mammals, yet swap their homes and neither would last a week. What sorts the living world into such different zones?
@goals
- Define a biome and explain what climate is.
- Describe the main land biomes by temperature, rainfall, plants and animals.
- Explain how latitude and altitude control where biomes occur.
- Explain how people have adapted to different biomes.
@section Climate decides
**Weather** is what the air is doing today. **Climate** is the pattern of weather over thirty years or more. A **biome** is a very large region with a similar climate and therefore similar plants and animals.
Two measurements matter most: **temperature** and **precipitation** (rain and snow). Plants respond to these, animals depend on the plants, and people depend on both.
@section The major land biomes
| Biome | Climate | Typical life |
| Tundra | Very cold, dry, frozen subsoil | Mosses, lichens, caribou, arctic fox |
| Taiga (boreal forest) | Long cold winters, short summers | Conifers, moose, wolves |
| Temperate forest | Four seasons, steady rain | Oak, maple, deer, bears |
| Grassland | Warm summers, too dry for forest | Grasses, bison, zebra, lions |
| Desert | Under 250 mm of rain a year | Cacti, succulents, reptiles, camels |
| Mediterranean scrub | Hot dry summers, mild wet winters | Olive, shrubs, goats |
| Tropical rainforest | Hot all year, over 2,000 mm of rain | The greatest variety of life on land |
Grasslands have many names: **savanna** in Africa, **steppe** in Asia, **prairie** in North America, **pampas** in South America. A desert is defined by dryness, not heat. Antarctica is the largest desert on Earth.
@section Why the biomes lie in bands
The Sun's rays strike the equator directly and the poles at a slant, so it is hot near 0° and cold near 90°. Biomes therefore run in broad bands by **latitude**: rainforest at the equator, deserts near 30°, forests in the middle latitudes, tundra near the poles.
**Altitude** copies latitude. Temperature drops about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m you climb. Walking up a tall tropical mountain is like travelling from the equator toward the pole: rainforest, then cloud forest, then grassland, then ice.
@section People and biomes
| Biome | A human adaptation |
| Desert | Oases, loose robes, travel by night, camels |
| Tundra | Fur clothing, hunting and herding in place of farming |
| Grassland | Herding on horseback; later, wheat and maize farming |
| Rainforest | Small shifting gardens, houses on stilts |
| River valleys in dry lands | Irrigation and the first cities |
The grasslands and river valleys became the world's breadbaskets. This is why the first civilisations, which you will meet from Week 5, rose where they did.
@example A place has an average temperature of 27 °C in every month and receives 2,600 mm of rain a year. Name its biome and give your reasons.
- The temperature is high and hardly changes, so it lies near the equator.
- 2,600 mm is above the 2,000 mm mark.
= Tropical rainforest: hot all year and very wet.
@activity Shoebox Biome and Climograph | a shoebox, natural and craft materials, graph paper, the monthly temperature and rainfall of one city (from an atlas or online)
- Choose one biome. Research three plants and three animals that live there.
- Build the biome inside the shoebox. Label one adaptation for each living thing.
- Draw a **climograph** for a city in that biome: months along the bottom, rainfall as bars, temperature as a line.
- On the back of the box, write how people traditionally built homes, dressed and found food there.
- Present it in two minutes without notes.
@practice
? What is the difference between weather and climate?
= Weather is the day-to-day state of the air. Climate is the average pattern over decades.
! One cold day is weather. A place being cold every winter for thirty years is climate.
? Which two measurements best predict a biome?
= Temperature and precipitation
! Together they decide which plants can grow, and the plants decide the rest of the food web.
? Why is Antarctica classed as a desert?
= It receives very little precipitation.
! A desert is defined by dryness, under about 250 mm a year, not by heat.
? A mountain on the equator has snow at its summit. Explain.
= Temperature falls with altitude, by about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m.
! At 5,000 m the air is more than 30 °C colder than at sea level, cold enough for permanent ice.
? Give the local name for grassland in Africa and in North America.
= Savanna and prairie
! The same biome occurs on several continents, and each language named its own.
? Why did herding, and not crop farming, develop first on the dry steppe?
= There is enough rain for grass but not for most crops.
! Animals can turn grass, which people cannot eat, into milk and meat, and herds can move to follow the rain.
@challenge
? Baja California and Florida lie at similar latitudes, yet one is mostly desert and the other is green and humid. Suggest why latitude alone does not decide the biome.
= Ocean currents, wind direction and mountains also control rainfall.
! A cold current runs along Baja's Pacific coast and cool air holds little moisture, while mountains block rain from the east. Florida is bathed by warm, wet air from the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic.
@vocab
Biome: A large region with similar climate, plants and animals.
Climate: The usual pattern of weather over many years.
Precipitation: Water that falls from the sky as rain, snow or hail.
Adaptation: A feature or behaviour that helps a living thing survive in its environment.
Altitude: Height above sea level.
Climograph: A chart showing a place's monthly temperature and rainfall.
@talk
- Which biome would be hardest for humans to live in without modern technology? Defend your choice.
- When people cut down a rainforest to plant crops, have they changed the biome or only the plants?
- If the climate warms, what should happen to the bands of biomes? Who would gain and who would lose?

@lesson 3 literature Greek & Roman Myth
@hook We call a weakness an "Achilles' heel", a long trip an "odyssey" and a huge ship "titanic". Why are stories told 2,800 years ago still hiding inside the words we use today?
@goals
- Explain what a myth is and what it did for the people who told it.
- Name the major Olympian gods by their Greek and Roman names.
- Retell three myths and state the idea each one teaches.
- Recognise and explain allusions to Greek myth.
@section What myths are for
A **myth** is a traditional story that a people told to explain the world and their place in it. The ancient Greeks had no microscopes or weather maps. Their myths answered the questions every people asks: Why do the seasons change? Where did fire come from? Why do proud people fall?
Today we read these stories as **literature**. Nobody worships Zeus, but writers, artists and scientists have borrowed from the myths for more than two thousand years. A reader who knows them holds a key to a great deal of later writing.
Myths do three jobs. They **explain** nature, they **teach** what a society values, and they **warn** about human faults.
@section The Olympians
The Romans adopted the Greek gods and gave them Latin names. Many planets carry the Roman names.
| Greek | Roman | Realm |
| Zeus | Jupiter | King of the gods, sky and thunder |
| Hera | Juno | Marriage, queen of the gods |
| Poseidon | Neptune | The sea |
| Hades | Pluto | The underworld |
| Athena | Minerva | Wisdom and craft |
| Apollo | Apollo | Light, music, prophecy |
| Artemis | Diana | The hunt, the moon |
| Ares | Mars | War |
| Aphrodite | Venus | Love and beauty |
| Hermes | Mercury | Messenger, travellers |
| Hephaestus | Vulcan | Fire and the forge |
| Demeter | Ceres | Grain and harvest |
The Greek gods are powerful but not good. They are jealous, proud and quarrelsome, like very strong human beings. This makes the myths studies of human character.
@section Three myths to know
**Demeter and Persephone.** Hades carries Persephone, daughter of the harvest goddess Demeter, to the underworld. Demeter grieves and nothing grows. Persephone is allowed to return, but because she ate pomegranate seeds below she must go back for part of each year. *Job: explains the seasons.*
**Prometheus.** The Titan Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humankind. Zeus chains him to a rock as punishment. *Job: explains how humans gained skill and craft, and asks what the price of progress is.*
**Daedalus and Icarus.** The inventor Daedalus builds wings of feathers and wax to escape from Crete. He warns his son Icarus not to fly too high. Icarus soars toward the sun, the wax melts, and he falls into the sea. *Job: warns against hubris.*
@section Hubris and nemesis
**Hubris** is pride so great that a person forgets their limits. In Greek stories hubris is always followed by **nemesis**, the downfall it deserves. Arachne boasts that she weaves better than Athena and is turned into a spider. King Midas wishes for everything he touches to become gold and nearly starves.
@section Allusions
An **allusion** is a brief reference to a well-known story. The writer expects you to recognise it.
| Allusion | Meaning | Source |
| Achilles' heel | One fatal weakness | Achilles could be wounded only in his heel |
| Pandora's box | An act that releases many troubles | Pandora opened a forbidden jar |
| Herculean task | A job needing enormous strength | The twelve labours of Hercules |
| Midas touch | A gift for making money | King Midas |
| An odyssey | A long wandering journey | Odysseus' ten-year voyage home |
| Narcissism | Excessive self-love | Narcissus fell in love with his reflection |
@example What does the writer mean? "The new software was a Pandora's box: once we installed it, error after error poured out."
- Identify the allusion: Pandora's box.
- Recall the myth: Pandora opened a container and troubles flew out into the world and could not be put back.
- Apply it: installing the software is the opening, and the errors are the troubles.
= Installing it caused many problems that could not easily be undone.
@activity Myth Retold | paper, pencils, any collection of Greek myths
- Read one myth not described above (try Perseus, Theseus, Orpheus or Atalanta).
- Fill in a four-box page: characters, the problem, the turning point, the ending.
- Decide which job the myth does: explain, teach or warn. Write one sentence of evidence.
- Retell it aloud in under three minutes without notes.
- Find one modern word, brand or space mission named after a figure from Greek or Roman myth.

@vocab

@lesson 3 languages Morphology 101
@hook You have probably never seen the word "unmicrowaveable", yet you understood it at once. How can you know the meaning of a word nobody ever taught you?
@goals
- Define morpheme, root, prefix and suffix.
- Split words into their morphemes.
- Tell free morphemes from bound ones.
- Use common prefixes and suffixes to work out unfamiliar words.
@section Words are built from parts
**Morphology** is the study of how words are built. The smallest piece of a word that carries meaning is a **morpheme**.
The word *unbreakable* has three morphemes: **un-** (not), **break**, and **-able** (can be). A morpheme is not the same as a syllable. *Elephant* has three syllables but only one morpheme, because "el", "e" and "phant" mean nothing alone. *Cats* has one syllable but two morphemes: **cat** and **-s** (more than one).
@section Free and bound
- A **free morpheme** can stand alone as a word: *break, kind, play*.
- A **bound morpheme** must be attached to something: *un-, -ness, -ed, -s*.
The **root** is the core that carries the main meaning. An **affix** is a bound morpheme added to a root. An affix placed in front is a **prefix**. One placed behind is a **suffix**.
@section The most useful prefixes and suffixes
| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
| un-, in-, im- | not | unkind, invisible, impossible |
| re- | again | rebuild |
| pre- | before | preview |
| mis- | wrongly | misspell |
| dis- | opposite of | disagree |
| sub- | under | submarine |
| inter- | between | international |
| trans- | across | transport |
| Suffix | Makes | Example |
| -er, -or | a person or thing that does | teacher, actor |
| -ness | a noun from an adjective | kindness |
| -ful / -less | full of / without | hopeful, hopeless |
| -able, -ible | can be | readable, visible |
| -tion, -sion | an action or result | creation, decision |
| -ly | how something is done | quickly |
@section Two kinds of suffix
An **inflectional** suffix changes the grammar of a word but not what kind of word it is: walk, walk**s**, walk**ed**, walk**ing**. A **derivational** affix builds a new word, often of a different kind: *kind* (adjective) becomes *kindness* (noun).
Other languages do the same. Spanish *-mente* matches English *-ly* (*rápidamente*, rapidly), and Spanish *des-* matches *dis-* (*desaparecer*, disappear). Morphology is a tool you can carry from language to language.
@example Split "reconstruction" into morphemes and work out its meaning.
- Prefix: re- (again).
- Prefix: con- (together).
- Root: struct (build), from Latin *struere*.
- Suffix: -ion (action or result).
= re + con + struct + ion: "the act of building together again"
@example How many morphemes are in "carpet"?
- Tempting split: car + pet.
- Test the meaning: a carpet has nothing to do with a car or a pet.
= One. A piece counts as a morpheme only if it carries its meaning into the word.
@activity The Word Factory | 30 small cards, three colours of pen
- Write ten prefixes in one colour, ten roots in another (act, form, port, view, play, kind, hope, read, build, agree) and ten suffixes in the third.
- Build as many real words as you can by laying cards side by side. Record each.
- Now build five words that do **not** exist but could, such as "unviewable" or "misbuilder". Write a dictionary definition for each.
- Sort your real words: which suffixes were inflectional and which derivational?
- Find the longest real word you can build from the cards.
@practice

@challenge

= inter + nation + al + ise + ation (five morphemes)

@vocab
Morphology: The study of how words are formed from meaningful parts.
Morpheme: The smallest meaningful part of a word.
Root: The core morpheme carrying a word's main meaning.
Affix: A bound morpheme attached to a root; a prefix or suffix.
Inflection: A change to a word that shows grammar, such as tense or number.
Derivation: Building a new word by adding an affix.
